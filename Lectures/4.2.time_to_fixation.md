# Time to Fixation During a Selective Sweep Using Odds

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students should be able to:

1. Define odds and distinguish them from probabilities.
2. Understand why selection acts multiplicatively on odds.
3. Derive allele frequency trajectories without calculus.
4. Calculate the time required for a beneficial allele to sweep through a population.
5. Understand why sweep duration depends on both selection and population size.

## Core concept:

*Selection changes allele frequencies in a multiplicative manner*, making it convenient to work with **odds** rather than frequencies themselves. The odds of an allele are defined as

$$
\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

where $p$ is the allele frequency. Under simple directional selection, the odds increase by approximately a factor of $1+s$ each generation, where $s$ is the selection coefficient. To estimate the time required for an allele to reach fixation, we calculate its initial and final odds, determine the total fold increase in odds required, and then solve for the number of generations needed for repeated multiplication by $1+s$ to produce that increase. This yields

$$
t=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p_1/(1-p_1)}{p_0/(1-p_0)}\right)}{\ln(1+s)}
$$

where $p_0$ and $p_1$ are the initial and final allele frequencies. Conceptually, this is analogous to compound interest: selection increases the odds by the same proportion each generation, and the time to fixation is the number of generations required for those cumulative increases to move the allele from its starting frequency to near fixation.

---

# 1. Why Use Odds?

Most derivations of selective sweeps use calculus.

However, there is a simpler approach that uses only algebra and exponential growth.

The key insight is:

> Natural selection acts multiplicatively on the odds of carrying a beneficial allele.

This is analogous to compound interest in finance.

---

# 2. What Are Odds?

We usually describe an allele using its frequency:

$$
p
$$

For example,

$$
p=0.20
$$

means that 20% of alleles in the population are the beneficial allele.

The remaining frequency is

$$
q=1-p
$$

so

$$
q=0.80
$$

---

## Converting Frequency to Odds

Odds compare the frequency of a beneficial allele to the frequency of all competing alleles.

The odds are defined as

$$
\text{Odds}=\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

---

### Example 1

Suppose

$$
p=0.20
$$

Then

$$
\text{Odds}=\frac{0.20}{0.80}=0.25
$$

Meaning:

> There is one copy of the beneficial allele for every four copies of the alternative allele.

---

### Example 2

Suppose

$$
p=0.50
$$

Then

$$
\text{Odds}=\frac{0.50}{0.50}=1
$$

Meaning:

> The two alleles occur equally often.

---

### Example 3

Suppose

$$
p=0.90
$$

Then

$$
\text{Odds}=\frac{0.90}{0.10}=9
$$

Meaning:

> The beneficial allele occurs nine times more often than the alternative allele.

---

## Recovering Frequency from Odds

If the odds are known,

$$
O=\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

then

$$
p=\frac{O}{1+O}
$$

This lets us convert back and forth between frequencies and odds.

---

# 3. Why Selection Acts on Odds

Assume a beneficial allele has fitness advantage

$$
1+s
$$

relative to the alternative allele, whose fitness is

$$
1
$$

Each generation, selection increases the representation of the beneficial allele by a factor of

$$
1+s
$$

relative to the alternative allele.

Therefore the odds change according to

$$
\text{Odds}_{t+1}=(1+s)\text{Odds}_{t}
$$

This is the key equation.

---

## Example

Suppose

$$
s=0.10
$$

and the current odds are

$$
1
$$

Then after one generation

$$
\text{Odds}_1=1.1
$$

After another generation

$$
\text{Odds}_2=1.1^2=1.21
$$

After three generations

$$
\text{Odds}_3=1.1^3=1.331
$$

The odds grow exponentially.

---

# 4. Selection Is Like Compound Interest

This is mathematically identical to compound interest.

Money grows according to

$$
M_t=M_0(1+r)^t
$$

where

- $M_0$ is the initial amount
- $r$ is the interest rate

Likewise, **allele odds** grow according to

$$
O_t=O_0(1+s)^t
$$

where

- $O_0$ is the initial odds
- $s$ is the selection coefficient

Natural selection is therefore a process of compounded growth.

---

# 5. Deriving the Allele Frequency Trajectory

Start with

$$
O_t=O_0(1+s)^t
$$

Substitute the definition of odds:

$$
\frac{p_t}{1-p_t}=\frac{p_0}{1-p_0}(1+s)^t
$$

This equation gives the frequency of a beneficial allele after any number of generations.

---

# 6. Solving for Time

Suppose we want to know how long it takes an allele to move from

$$
p_0
$$

to

$$
p_1
$$

Start with

$$
\frac{p_1}{1-p_1}=\frac{p_0}{1-p_0}(1+s)^t
$$

Divide both sides by

$$
\frac{p_0}{1-p_0}
$$

to isolate the growth term:

$$
(1+s)^t=\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}
$$

Now take the logarithm of both sides:

$$
t\ln(1+s)=\ln\left(\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\right)
$$

Solving for time:

$$
t=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\right)}{\ln(1+s)}
$$

This is the fixation-time equation, derived!! *huzzah!* 

---

# 7. Example Sweep

Suppose a beneficial allele increases from

$$
p_0=0.01
$$

to

$$
p_1=0.99
$$

under

$$
s=0.10
$$

First calculate the odds ratio:

$$
\frac{0.99(0.99)}{0.01(0.01)}=9801
$$

Then

$$
t=\frac{\ln(9801)}{\ln(1.10)}
$$

Substituting values:

$$
t=\frac{9.19}{0.0953}
$$

giving

$$
t\approx96\text{ generations}
$$

---

# 8. A New Mutation

A newly arising mutation begins as a single copy in a diploid population.

Since there are

$$
2N
$$

gene copies in the population, its initial frequency is

$$
p_0 = \frac{1}{2N}
$$

For a population of one million individuals,

$$
N = 10^6
$$

so

$$
p_0=\frac{1}{2\times10^6}=5\times10^{-7}
$$

This means that only

$$
0.00005\%
$$

of gene copies initially carry the mutation.

---


## Step 1: Convert Frequency to Odds

Natural selection acts multiplicatively on **odds**, not directly on frequencies.

Odds are defined as

$$
\text{Odds}=\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

For the new mutation,

$$
\text{Odds}_0=
\frac{5\times10^{-7}}
{1-5\times10^{-7}}
$$

Because the mutation is extremely rare,

$$
1-5\times10^{-7}
\approx 1
$$

so

$$
\text{Odds}_0
\approx 5\times10^{-7}
$$

Thus the mutation starts with odds of roughly

$$
5\times10^{-7}
$$

or about

$$
1 : 2,000,000
$$

against fixation.

---

## Step 2: Define the Endpoint

Fixation technically occurs at

$$
p=1
$$

but the odds become infinite at that point.

To avoid infinities, we use

$$
p_1=0.99
$$

which is effectively fixation.

The final odds are

$$
\text{Odds}_1=
\frac{0.99}{1-0.99}=
\frac{0.99}{0.01}=
99
$$

So by the end of the sweep the odds are

$$
99
$$

or approximately

$$
99:1
$$

in favor of the allele.

---

## Step 3: Calculate How Much the Odds Must Increase

Selection must increase the odds from

$$
5\times10^{-7}
$$

to

$$
99
$$

The required fold change is

$$
\frac{\text{Odds}_1}{\text{Odds}_0}=
\frac{99}{5\times10^{-7}}
$$

Thus

$$
\frac{\text{Odds}_1}{\text{Odds}_0}=
1.98\times10^8\approx 2\times10^8
$$

Thus the odds must increase about

$$
200,000,000
$$

fold.

This enormous number reflects the fact that a successful mutation starts at an extremely low frequency.

---

## Step 4: Relate Odds Growth to Selection

For a beneficial allele with selection coefficient

$$
s
$$

the odds increase approximately by a factor

$$
(1+s)
$$

every generation.

If

$$
s=0.01
$$

then each generation multiplies the odds by

$$
1.01
$$

After one generation:

$$
\text{Odds}=\text{Odds}_0(1.01)
$$

After two generations:

$$
\text{Odds}=
\text{Odds}_0(1.01)^2
$$

After three generations:

$$
\text{Odds}=
\text{Odds}_0(1.01)^3
$$

Continuing for

$$
t
$$

generations gives

$$
\text{Odds}_t=
\text{Odds}_0(1.01)^t
$$

Since we know the odds must increase by a factor of

$$
2\times10^8
$$

we set

$$
(1.01)^t=2\times10^8
$$

---

## Step 5: Solve for the Number of Generations

Take the natural logarithm of both sides:

$$
\ln\!\left((1.01)^t\right)=\ln(2\times10^8)
$$

Use the logarithm rule

$$
\ln(a^b)=b\ln(a)
$$

to obtain

$$
t\ln(1.01)=
\ln(2\times10^8)
$$

Now divide both sides by

$$
\ln(1.01)
$$

to isolate

$$
t
$$

do

$$
t=\frac{\ln(2\times10^8)}
{\ln(1.01)}
$$

---

## Step 6: Compute the Numbers

First compute the numerator:

$$
\ln(2\times10^8)=
\ln(2)+\ln(10^8)
=19.11
$$

Next compute the denominator:

$$
\ln(1.01)=0.00995
$$

Substituting:

$$
t=\frac{19.11}{0.00995}
$$

$$
t\approx1920
\text{ generations}
$$

---

## Interpretation

A mutation with a modest selective advantage of

$$
s=0.01
$$

must increase its odds by roughly

$$
200\text{ million}
$$

fold to go from a single copy in a population of one million individuals to near fixation.

Because selection increases odds by only about

$$
1\%
$$

per generation, it takes approximately

$$
1900
$$

generations for the allele to complete this sweep.

This illustrates a key result of population genetics:

> Even strongly beneficial mutations begin extraordinarily rare, so most of the time required for fixation is spent escaping low frequencies.
---

# 9. Generalizing the behavior of new mutations
Starting from

$$
t=
\frac{
\ln
\left(
\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}
{p_0(1-p_1)}
\right)
}{
\ln(1+s)
}
$$

we make a series of biologically reasonable approximations.

---

## Step 1: Assume a New Mutation

For a new beneficial mutation in a diploid population,

$$
p_0=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

and fixation is approximated as

$$
p_1 \approx 1-\frac{1}{2N}.
$$

Therefore

$$
1-p_0=
1-\frac{1}{2N}
$$

and

$$
1-p_1=
\frac{1}{2N}.
$$

Substituting into the equation gives

$$
t=
\frac{
\ln
\left(
\frac{
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
}
{
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)
}
\right)
}{
\ln(1+s)
}.
$$

---

## Step 2: Expand the Fraction

The denominator is

$$
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)^2=
\frac{1}{4N^2}.
$$

Thus

$$
t=
\frac{
\ln
\left[
4N^2
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)^2
\right]
}{
\ln(1+s)
}.
$$

---

## Step 3: Large Population Approximation

For large populations,

$$
N \gg 1,
$$

so

$$
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\approx 1.
$$

Therefore

$$
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)^2
\approx 1
$$

and

$$
t
\approx
\frac{\ln(4N^2)}
{\ln(1+s)}.
$$

---

## Step 4: Simplify the Logarithm

Using

$$
\ln(ab)=\ln(a)+\ln(b)
$$

and

$$
\ln(x^2)=2\ln(x),
$$

we obtain

$$
\ln(4N^2)=
\ln(4)+2\ln(N).
$$

Since

$$
\ln(4)=2\ln(2),
$$

this becomes

$$
\ln(4N^2)=
2\ln(2N).
$$

Therefore

$$
t\approx
\frac{2\ln(2N)}
{\ln(1+s)}.
$$

---

## Step 5: Weak Selection Approximation

When selection is weak,

$$
s \ll 1,
$$

the Taylor expansion gives

$$
\ln(1+s)
\approx s.
$$

Substituting,

$$
t
\approx
\frac{2\ln(2N)}
{s}.
$$

This shows that fixation time increases only logarithmically with population size but decreases inversely with the strength of selection.


# 10. Why Larger Populations Require More Time

A new mutation always begins as a single copy. Thus, notice two important relationships:

$$
t\propto\frac{1}{s}
$$

and

$$
t\propto\ln(N)
$$

Therefore:

- Stronger selection produces faster sweeps.
- Larger populations produce slightly longer sweeps.
- Population size affects fixation time only logarithmically.

---

# Visual Interpretation

Selection repeatedly multiplies allele odds by

$$
(1+s)
$$

each generation:

$$
O_0 \rightarrow O_0 (1+s)\rightarrow O_0 (1+s)^2\rightarrow O_0 (1+s)^3 \rightarrow ...
$$

As the odds increase exponentially, allele frequency follows an S-shaped trajectory (that we derived last class):

$$
0
\rightarrow
\text{slow}
\rightarrow
\text{fast}
\rightarrow
\text{slow}
\rightarrow
1
$$

This produces the characteristic selective sweep curve.

$$
p=\frac{O}{1+O}
$$

### Growth of odds under selection

$$

$$

$$
t=
\frac{

\right)

}
$$

### Weak-selection approximation

$$
t
\approx
\frac{1}{s}
\ln
\left(
\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}

\right)
$$

### Sweep duration from a new mutation

$$
t
\approx
\frac{2\ln(2N)}

$$

# Take-Home Message

The key idea is that selection multiplies the odds of a beneficial allele by a factor of

$$
1+s
$$

every generation. This makes allele evolution mathematically equivalent to compound interest: the expected duration of a selective sweep follows directly from algebra and exponential growth.

# Introduction

Most mutations are neutral or deleterious.

Occasionally, a mutation increases fitness.

When this occurs, natural selection increases the frequency of the beneficial allele.

This process is called

$$
\text{Directional Selection}
$$

because one allele is consistently favored over another.

When a beneficial mutation rapidly rises in frequency and reaches fixation, the event is called a

$$
\text{Selective Sweep}
$$

Selective sweeps leave characteristic signatures in genomic data that can persist long after fixation.

---

# Directional Selection at a Single Locus

Consider a beneficial allele

$$
A_i
$$

competing against an ancestral allele

$$
A_j
$$

Suppose fitnesses are

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|-----------|
| $A_iA_i$ | $1+s$ |
| $A_iA_j$ | $1+hs$ |
| $A_jA_j$ | $1$ |

where

- $s$ is the selection coefficient
- $h$ is the dominance coefficient

If

$$
s > 0
$$

then allele $A_i$ is beneficial.

---

# Allele Frequency Change Under Selection

Let

$$
p
$$

be the frequency of the beneficial allele.

Directional selection causes $p$ to increase each generation.

A typical trajectory resembles

```
Frequency
1.0 |                   _______
    |                __/
    |             __/
    |          __/
    |       __/
    |    __/
    | __/
0.0 +--------------------------------
```

Time

Three phases are typically observed:

1. Initial stochastic phase

2. Rapid deterministic increase

3. Approach to fixation

# What Happens to Nearby Neutral Variants?

Suppose a beneficial mutation arises on a chromosome carrying a particular haplotype:

```
A C G T A
    *
Beneficial 
Mutation
```

Selection acts directly on the beneficial allele.

However, nearby variants are physically linked to the selected site. As the beneficial mutation rises in frequency, nearby neutral variants rise in frequency as well.

This process is called

$$
\text{Genetic Hitchhiking}
$$

---

# Genetic Hitchhiking

Imagine the beneficial mutation appears on the haplotype

$$
A_iB_iC_iD_i
$$

Initially:

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $A_iB_iC_iD_i$ | Very Rare |
| Other Haplotypes | Common |

As selection proceeds:

| Generation | Frequency of Selected Haplotype |
|------------|-------------------------------|
| 0 | 0.1% |
| 50 | 10% |
| 100 | 95% |

Nearby linked variants are "carried" by the beneficial mutation toward fixation.

This is the essence of genetic hitchhiking.

---

# The Concept of a Selective Sweep

A selective sweep occurs when

1. A beneficial mutation arises.

2. Positive selection increases its frequency.

3. The beneficial mutation approaches fixation.

4. Linked genetic variation hitchhikes with it.

The result is a dramatic alteration of local patterns of genetic variation.

When selection drives it toward fixation, all surviving chromosomes near the selected site trace back to that original chromosome.

Consequently, genealogies become unusually shallow.

This produces:


- Reduced genetic diversity
- Increased haplotype similarity

---

# Genomic Footprint 1: Reduction in Nucleotide Diversity

```
Before a sweep:

Chr1 A T G C G A
Chr2 A C G C A A
Chr3 T T G T G A
Chr4 A C A C G T

After a sweep:

Chr1 A C G C G A
Chr2 A C G C G A
Chr3 A C G C G A
Chr4 A C G C G A
```

Most local variation disappears.

Therefore nucleotide diversity

$$
\pi
$$

drops around the selected locus.

### Why Diversity Decreases

The beneficial mutation originated on one chromosome. After fixation, most sampled chromosomes descend from that original haplotype. This resembles a localized population bottleneck.

Consequences include:

- Reduced heterozygosity
- Reduced nucleotide diversity
- Reduced haplotype diversity
- Reduced local effective population size

---

# Genomic Footprint 2: Distortion of the Site Frequency Spectrum

A sweep removes standing variation.
After fixation, new mutations begin accumulating again.
Thus the region becomes enriched for:

- Singletons
- Doubletons
- Rare variants

and depleted for intermediate-frequency variants.
This distorts the site frequency spectrum.

---

# Genomic Footprint 3: Long Haplotypes

Selection occurs rapidly.

Recombination often cannot break apart the chromosome carrying the beneficial mutation quickly enough.

Consequently many individuals share large blocks of identical sequence.

Example:

```
AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA
AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA
AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA
AAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAAA
```

These extended shared haplotypes are a hallmark of recent positive selection.


### The Role of Recombination

Recombination erodes sweep signatures.

Suppose the selected mutation occurs at

```
-----------------*----------------
                 ^
           Selected Site
```

Sites very close to the selected mutation experience little recombination.

Sites farther away experience more recombination.

Thus sweep signatures become weaker with increasing distance from the selected site.

---

# Genomic Footprint 4: Elevated Linkage Disequilibrium

The sweep drags an entire chromosomal segment through the population.

Nearby loci become highly associated.

Therefore LD increases.

This produces elevated values of

$$
D
$$

and

$$
r^2
$$

around the selected site.

---

# Recovery Following a Sweep

Following fixation:

- New mutations introduce variation.

- Recombination breaks down long haplotypes.

- LD gradually decays.

- Diversity begins recovering.

Thus sweep signatures weaken through time.

Recent sweeps are easier to detect than ancient sweeps.

---

# Hard Sweeps

A hard sweep begins with a single new advantageous mutation.

Process:

```
New Mutation
     |
     v
Selection
     |
     v
Rapid Increase
     |
     v
Fixation
```

Characteristics:

- Strong reduction in diversity

- One dominant haplotype

- Strong LD signature

- Obvious genomic footprint

---

# Soft Sweeps

Selection does not always begin from a new mutation.

Sometimes adaptive alleles already exist in the population before environmental change.

This is adaptation from standing genetic variation.

---

# Genomic Consequences of Soft Sweeps

Because multiple haplotypes already carry the adaptive allele:

- Diversity reduction is weaker

- Multiple haplotypes remain common

- LD signatures are weaker

- Long haplotypes are less pronounced

Soft sweeps are therefore more difficult to detect.

---

# Hard Versus Soft Sweeps

| Feature | Hard Sweep | Soft Sweep |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Origin | New mutation | Standing variation |
| Number of Origins | One | Multiple |
| Dominant Haplotype | One | Several |
| Diversity Reduction | Strong | Weaker |
| LD Signal | Strong | Moderate |
| Detection | Easier | Harder |

---

# Recombination Determines Sweep Size

| Recombination Rate | Sweep Footprint | Mechanism | Consequence |
|-------------------|----------------|-----------|-------------|
| Low | Wide | Linkage persists over long distances | Selection affects large genomic regions |
| High | Narrow | Recombination breaks associations between loci | Selection effects remain localized around the target site |

### Visual Representation

```text

Low recombination:

<---------------------------->

Wide sweep footprint

High recombination:

<---->

Narrow sweep footprint

```

**Key idea:** Recombination determines how far the effects of positive selection extend across the genome.

---

# Strong Selection Produces Strong Signatures

| Selection Coefficient (*s*) | Fixation Speed | Time for Recombination | Expected Signature |
|-----------------------------|----------------|-----------------------|-------------------|
| Large | Fast | Little | Strong sweep signature |
| Small | Slow | More | Weak sweep signature |

### Consequences of Strong Selection

- Longer haplotypes

- Stronger linkage disequilibrium (LD)

- Larger reductions in nucleotide diversity

- More obvious selective sweep signatures

### Mechanism

Large values of

$$
s
$$

cause beneficial alleles to reach fixation rapidly. Because fixation occurs quickly, recombination has less opportunity to break down linkage between the selected allele and nearby neutral variants. As a result, stronger selection produces larger and more detectable genomic signatures of adaptation.




# Big Picture

A selective sweep occurs when directional selection drives a beneficial mutation through a population.

Because neighboring loci are linked to that mutation, they hitchhike along with it.

The resulting genomic footprint consists of:

1. Reduced diversity.

2. Elevated linkage disequilibrium.

3. Long shared haplotypes.

4. Distorted site-frequency spectra.

5. Characteristic valleys of diversity surrounding selected sites.

Modern population genomics relies heavily on identifying these signatures to reconstruct the recent adaptive history of populations.

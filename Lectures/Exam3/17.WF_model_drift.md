# Introduction: What Is Missing From Our Previous Models?

In previous lectures, we developed models for mutation and natural selection.

Although these evolutionary forces are biologically different, the mathematical models shared an important characteristic: they were largely **deterministic**.

A deterministic model is one in which the future can be predicted exactly from the current conditions.

If we know:

- allele frequencies,
- mutation rates,
- genotype fitnesses,

then we can calculate exactly what allele frequencies will be next generation.

This predictability arises because our models implicitly assume that populations are effectively infinite.

Under an infinite-population assumption:

- allele frequencies are represented perfectly,
- every generation samples the parental population perfectly,
- sampling error does not exist.

This assumption is mathematically convenient.

Unfortunately it is biologically unrealistic.

No biological population is infinite.

Even populations consisting of millions of individuals contain only a finite number of gene copies.

Once populations become finite, reproduction ceases to be a perfect process.

Instead, reproduction becomes a sampling process.

The next generation represents only a sample of the alleles currently present.

And whenever sampling occurs, chance enters the system.

That chance component of evolution is called **genetic drift**.

Genetic drift is therefore the evolutionary consequence of finite population size.

---

# Revisiting the Assumptions of Earlier Models

Our mutation-selection models generally assumed:

- Individuals are diploid.
- We track one locus.
- Allele frequencies sum to one.
- No new mutations occur during the modeled interval.
- No migration occurs.
- No predators or ecological factors alter genotype fitness.
- No mate choice occurs.
- Populations are effectively infinite.

Today we relax only one assumption:

**Populations are finite.**

Nothing else needs to change.

Even if:

- all alleles have identical fitness,
- no mutation occurs,
- no migration occurs,

allele frequencies can still evolve.

Why?

Because finite populations must sample alleles from generation to generation.

Genetic drift emerges automatically from this sampling process.

---

# What Is Genetic Drift?

Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies caused by finite population size.

Unlike natural selection:

- Drift is not adaptive.
- Drift does not consistently favor one allele over another.
- Drift results entirely from random sampling.

Drift is therefore a stochastic evolutionary process.

---

# Evolutionary Sampling

To understand drift, consider an analogy from statistics.

Suppose a giant container contains one million M&Ms.

Assume the true color frequencies are:

- 20% blue
- 30% orange
- 50% brown

Now sample ten M&Ms.

Will the sample contain exactly:

- 2 blue,
- 3 orange,
- 5 brown?

Almost certainly not.

The sample is only an estimate of the true population frequencies.

Random deviations occur because the sample size is finite.

The same idea applies to alleles.

The parental population contains the "true" allele frequencies.

The offspring generation represents only a sample of those alleles.

Therefore offspring frequencies rarely match parental frequencies perfectly.

The discrepancy between expected and realized frequencies is genetic drift.

---

# Deterministic Versus Stochastic Evolution

Selection is generally deterministic.

Suppose allele $\alpha_i$ has higher fitness than allele $\alpha_j$.

$$
w_i > w_j
$$

Under selection, allele $\alpha_i$ is expected to increase.

The direction of evolutionary change is predictable.

Genetic drift behaves differently.

Drift is a stochastic process.

A stochastic process is one in which outcomes depend upon probability distributions.

This does not mean drift is completely unpredictable.

Instead:

- We cannot predict the exact outcome.
- We can predict the probabilities of possible outcomes.

The major goal of the Wright-Fisher model is therefore not to predict a single future allele frequency.

Instead it predicts the distribution of possible future allele frequencies.

---

# A Simple Sampling Example

Consider a diploid population containing

$$
N = 10
$$

individuals.

Each diploid individual contains two copies of the locus.

Therefore:

$$
2N = 20
$$

gene copies exist in the population.

Suppose:

$$
p = 0.50
$$

for allele $\alpha_i$.

The population contains:

$$
20(0.50)=10
$$

copies of allele $\alpha_i$.

Now imagine producing the next generation by randomly sampling twenty gametes.

Must we obtain exactly ten copies of $\alpha_i$?

No.

Possible outcomes include:

| Copies of $\alpha_i$ | Frequency |
|----------|----------|
| 8 | 0.40 |
| 9 | 0.45 |
| 10 | 0.50 |
| 11 | 0.55 |
| 12 | 0.60 |

Notice what happened.

No selection occurred.

No mutation occurred.

No migration occurred.

Yet allele frequencies changed.

The only cause was random sampling.

This is genetic drift.

---

# Coin-Flipping Analogy

A useful analogy is coin flipping.

A fair coin has

$$
P(\text{Heads}) = 0.5
$$

If we flip the coin 20 times, the expected number of heads is

$$
20(0.5)=10
$$

However, we rarely observe exactly ten heads.

Possible outcomes include:

- 8 heads
- 11 heads
- 13 heads

The expected outcome is known.

The realized outcome varies.

This variation arises entirely because sampling is random.

Allele sampling behaves exactly the same way.

---

# Why Population Size Matters

Suppose we compare two samples:

- 10 alleles
- 10,000 alleles

Which sample should be more representative of the parental population?

The larger sample.

Larger samples reduce sampling error.

Consequently:

$$
\text{Strength of Drift} \propto \frac{1}{N}
$$

This relationship is one of the central ideas of genetic drift.

Small populations experience strong drift.

Large populations experience weak drift.

---

# Three Important Observations from Simulations

Before deriving equations, simulations reveal three important patterns.

These patterns will later emerge naturally from Wright-Fisher mathematics.

---

# Observation 1: Smaller Populations Experience More Drift

Repeated simulations show that allele frequencies fluctuate more strongly in smaller populations.

To measure one generation of drift, define

$$
\Delta_D p = p_{t+1}-p_t
$$

where:

- $p_t$ is the current allele frequency,
- $p_{t+1}$ is the next-generation allele frequency.

This quantity measures the amount of change generated purely by drift.

Consider repeated simulations that all begin at

$$
p=0.5
$$

Some outcomes may be:

| Current $p$ | Next $p$ | $\Delta_D p$ |
|------------|------------|------------|
| 0.50 | 0.49 | -0.01 |
| 0.50 | 0.58 | +0.08 |
| 0.50 | 0.46 | -0.04 |
| 0.50 | 0.41 | -0.09 |
| 0.50 | 0.54 | +0.04 |
| 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.00 |

The average change is approximately zero.

However the magnitude of fluctuations depends strongly on population size.

Small populations experience larger fluctuations because every sampled allele contributes a larger fraction of the gene pool.

---

# Observation 2: Extinction Depends on Current Frequency

Simulations also reveal that common alleles survive more often than rare alleles.

An allele beginning at

$$
p=0.01
$$

usually disappears.

An allele beginning at

$$
p=0.80
$$

often fixes.

This observation eventually leads to one of the most elegant results in population genetics:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=p
$$

The fixation probability of a neutral allele equals its current frequency.

We will derive this result later.

---

# Observation 3: Drift Removes Genetic Variation

Simulation trajectories repeatedly approach two special boundaries:

$$
p=0
$$

and

$$
p=1
$$

These are called absorbing boundaries.

If:

$$
p=0
$$

the allele has been lost.

If:

$$
p=1
$$

the allele has fixed.

Because neither mutation nor migration exists in the model, once an absorbing boundary is reached there is no mechanism for returning.

Drift therefore tends to eliminate variation through time.

---

# The Wright-Fisher Model

The Wright-Fisher model is the simplest mathematical model of drift.

It was developed independently by:

- Ronald Fisher
- Sewall Wright

The Wright-Fisher framework serves as the fundamental null model of population genetics.

Every major theory in modern population genetics ultimately traces back to this model.

---

# Assumptions of the Wright-Fisher Model

The model assumes:

- Diploid population.
- Constant population size.
- Random mating.
- Non-overlapping generations.
- No mutation.
- No migration.
- No selection.
- Random sampling of gametes.

Under these assumptions, drift is the only evolutionary force.

---

# Wright-Fisher Reproduction

Let

$$
p_t
$$

denote the frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ at generation $t$.

The parental generation contains

$$
2N
$$

gene copies.

The next generation is formed by sampling

$$
2N
$$

gametes from the parental gamete pool.

Each sampling event is independent.

Each sampled gamete carries allele $\alpha_i$ with probability

$$
p_t
$$

and carries allele $\alpha_j$ with probability

$$
1-p_t
$$

---

# Deriving the Wright-Fisher Sampling Distribution

Our objective is to calculate the probability that exactly $k$ copies of allele $\alpha_i$ appear in the next generation.

Notice the structure of the problem.

We perform:

$$
2N
$$

independent sampling events.

Each event can produce:

- success (allele $\alpha_i$)
- failure (allele $\alpha_j$)

The probability of success equals

$$
p_t
$$

Therefore this is a binomial experiment.

Let:

$$
X
$$

denote the number of copies of allele $\alpha_i$ sampled into generation $t+1$.

Then:

$$
X \sim \text{Binomial}(2N,p_t)
$$

The probability of observing exactly $k$ copies is therefore

$$
P(X=k)=\binom{2N}{k}p_t^k(1-p_t)^{2N-k}
$$

This is the central equation of the Wright-Fisher model.

Everything else follows from this distribution.

---

# Example Calculation

Suppose:

$$
N=5
$$

and

$$
p_t=0.5
$$

Then:

$$
2N=10
$$

gene copies are sampled.

What is the probability of obtaining exactly six copies of allele $\alpha_i$?

Using the binomial distribution:

$$
P(X=6)=\binom{10}{6}(0.5)^6(0.5)^4
$$

Since

$$
\binom{10}{6}=210
$$

we obtain

$$
P(X=6)=210(0.5)^{10}
$$

Since

$$
(0.5)^{10}=0.0009766
$$

we obtain

$$
P(X=6)\approx0.205
$$

There is therefore approximately a 20.5% chance of obtaining six copies.

---

# Deriving the Expected Allele Frequency

A common question is:

Does drift push allele frequencies upward or downward on average?

To answer this question we calculate the expectation.

For a binomial random variable,

$$
E[X]=2Np_t
$$

The next-generation allele frequency is

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{X}{2N}
$$

Taking expectations,

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=E\left(\frac{X}{2N}\right)
$$

Because expectation is linear,

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=\frac{1}{2N}E[X]
$$

Substituting the binomial mean:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=\frac{1}{2N}(2Np_t)
$$

The factors cancel:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=p_t
$$

This result is extremely important.

Drift changes allele frequencies.

But drift produces no directional bias.

On average:

$$
E[\Delta p]=0
$$

Drift is unbiased.

---

# Deriving the Variance Generated by Drift

Although the mean does not change, drift generates variance.

This variance is what causes populations to diverge from one another.

For a binomial random variable:

$$
\text{Var}(X)=2Np_t(1-p_t)
$$

Because

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{X}{2N}
$$

we have

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\text{Var}\left(\frac{X}{2N}\right)
$$

Using the variance rule

$$
\text{Var}(aX)=a^2\text{Var}(X)
$$

gives

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{1}{4N^2}\text{Var}(X)
$$

Substituting the binomial variance:

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{1}{4N^2}[2Np_t(1-p_t)]
$$

Simplifying:

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N}
$$

This equation quantifies the strength of drift.

Notice immediately:

- Large $N$ produces small variance.
- Small $N$ produces large variance.

Thus drift strength is controlled by population size.

---

# When Is Drift Strongest?

Variance depends upon

$$
p(1-p)
$$

Consider some values:

| $p$ | $p(1-p)$ |
|-------|-------|
| 0.1 | 0.09 |
| 0.2 | 0.16 |
| 0.5 | 0.25 |
| 0.8 | 0.16 |
| 0.9 | 0.09 |

The maximum occurs at:

$$
p=0.5
$$

Consequently drift produces its largest fluctuations at intermediate frequencies.

---

# Genetic Drift as a Random Walk

We have shown:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=p_t
$$

and

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N}
$$

The first equation tells us there is no bias.

The second tells us random fluctuations occur every generation.

Together these imply that allele frequencies perform a random walk.

<br>

```text
Allele Frequency

1.0 |
0.9 |                    *
0.8 |
0.7 |                *
0.6 |             *
0.5 | *   *  *
0.4 |      *   *
0.3 |
0.2 |
0.1 |
0.0 +---------------------
      Generation
```

<br>

At every generation an allele may:

- increase,
- decrease,
- remain roughly unchanged.

Chance determines the exact trajectory.

---

# Replicate Populations

Suppose ten populations all begin with:

$$
p_0=0.50
$$

Although every population starts identically, drift causes trajectories to diverge.

Chance alone generates differences among populations.

One of the major signatures of drift is therefore increasing variance among populations.

---

# Why Fixation Is Inevitable

Allele frequencies are constrained by:

$$
0 \le p \le 1
$$

The boundaries

$$
p=0
$$

and

$$
p=1
$$

are absorbing states.

Because drift behaves as a random walk, allele frequencies continue wandering until one of these boundaries is reached.

Thus every neutral allele is eventually:

- lost, or
- fixed.

Drift inevitably removes variation.

---

# Deriving the Probability of Fixation

Let:

$$
u
$$

denote the probability that a neutral allele eventually fixes.

Eventually every replicate population reaches one of two outcomes:

- fixation ($p=1$),
- loss ($p=0$).

The average final frequency must equal the initial frequency because drift is unbiased.

Therefore:

$$
p=(1)(u)+(0)(1-u)
$$

Simplifying:

$$
p=u
$$

Therefore:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=p
$$

The probability of fixation equals the allele's current frequency.

---

# New Mutations

Suppose a new mutation appears in a single copy.

The population contains

$$
2N
$$

gene copies.

Its initial frequency is therefore

$$
p=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Substituting into the fixation probability:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Most new neutral mutations are therefore lost.

Only a very small fraction fix.

---

# Measuring Genetic Variation

To quantify variation we use heterozygosity.

For a two-allele locus:

$$
H=2pq
$$

where:

- $p$ is the frequency of $\alpha_i$,
- $q$ is the frequency of $\alpha_j$.

Heterozygosity is the probability that two randomly sampled alleles differ.

Our goal is to determine how drift changes $H$ through time.

---

# Identity By State Versus Identity By Descent

Before deriving heterozygosity dynamics, we must distinguish two concepts.

## Identity by State

Two alleles are identical by state if they possess the same sequence.

Example:

ACCT

ACCT

These alleles are identical by state.

## Identity by Descent

Two alleles are identical by descent if they share a common ancestral copy.

Consider:

Parent:

ACCT

Child:

ACCT

These alleles are:

- identical by state,
- identical by descent.

Now suppose mutation occurs.

Parent:

ACCT

Descendant:

ACCG

These alleles are no longer identical by state.

However they remain identical by descent because one descended from the other.

Identity by descent is the key concept required to understand drift.

---

# Deriving the Loss of Heterozygosity

This is one of the most important derivations in population genetics.

Before we begin, ask:

What are we trying to calculate?

We wish to understand how quickly drift eliminates genetic variation.

Directly calculating heterozygosity is difficult.

Instead we first calculate its opposite.

Define:

$$
G_t
$$

as the probability that two sampled alleles are identical.

This quantity is homozygosity.

Because every pair of alleles must be either:

- identical,
- different,

we have

$$
G_t+H_t=1
$$

Therefore:

$$
G_t=1-H_t
$$

and

$$
H_t=1-G_t
$$

Our strategy will be:

1. Derive a recursion for $G$.
2. Convert that recursion into a formula for $H$.

---

# Step 1: Sample Two Alleles

Imagine drawing two alleles from generation $t+1$.

We ask:

"What is the probability that these alleles are identical?"

There are two mutually exclusive routes.

---

# Route 1: Same Parent Copy

The first offspring allele chooses a parental copy.

There are

$$
2N
$$

possible parental copies.

For the second offspring allele to choose exactly the same parental copy, the probability is

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

If this occurs, the offspring alleles are automatically identical.

Therefore this route contributes

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

to homozygosity.

---

# Route 2: Different Parent Copies

The probability that the second offspring chooses a different parental copy is

$$
1-\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Now identity is not guaranteed.

The offspring alleles will be identical only if the parental alleles were already identical.

That probability is

$$
G_t
$$

Applying the multiplication rule for probabilities:

$$
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)G_t
$$

---

# Step 2: Combine Both Routes

The total probability of homozygosity equals the sum of the two mutually exclusive routes.

Therefore:

$$
G_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)G_t
$$

This recursion describes the accumulation of identity by descent under drift.

Notice the biological interpretation.

Drift does not increase homozygosity because it prefers certain alleles.

Instead repeated sampling repeatedly copies the same ancestral lineages.

As distinct lineages disappear, diversity declines.

---

# Step 3: Convert Homozygosity Into Heterozygosity

Substitute:

$$
G_t=1-H_t
$$

and

$$
G_{t+1}=1-H_{t+1}
$$

into the recursion.

This gives:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)(1-H_t)
$$

Expand:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+1-\frac{1}{2N}-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Cancel terms:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=1-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Subtract one from both sides:

$$
-H_{t+1}=-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Multiply by negative one:

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t-\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Factor out $H_t$:

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

This is one of the most important equations in population genetics.

---

# Interpreting the Heterozygosity Recursion

The recursion says that each generation removes the fraction

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

of the heterozygosity that remains.

Consequently:

- Small populations lose variation rapidly.
- Large populations lose variation slowly.

This mathematical result explains precisely why simulation trajectories eventually reach fixation or extinction.

---

# Heterozygosity Through Time

Apply the recursion repeatedly.

After one generation:

$$
H_1=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

After two generations:

$$
H_2=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^2
$$

Continuing for $t$ generations:

$$
H_t=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^t
$$

This equation describes the loss of neutral genetic variation through time under drift.

---

# Consequences of Genetic Drift

Genetic drift causes:

- Random allele-frequency change.
- Divergence among populations.
- Fixation and loss of alleles.
- Increased homozygosity.
- Reduced heterozygosity.
- Loss of genetic variation.
- Increased genetic differentiation among populations.

These effects become particularly strong when populations are small.

---

# Big Picture

The Wright-Fisher model describes evolution as a sampling process in finite populations.

Under this model:

- Alleles are randomly sampled between generations.
- Drift changes allele frequencies purely by chance.
- Expected allele frequencies remain unchanged.
- Variance accumulates through time.
- Allele frequencies follow random walks.
- Every neutral allele eventually reaches fixation or loss.
- Heterozygosity declines every generation.
- Genetic variation is steadily eliminated.

The Wright-Fisher model serves as the foundation for:

- Effective population size.
- Inbreeding theory.
- Coalescent theory.
- Neutral theory.
- Molecular evolution.
- Population genomics.

Virtually all modern population genetic theory begins with the Wright-Fisher framework.

# Introduction: What Is Missing From Our Previous Models?

In previous lectures we developed mathematical models of mutation and natural selection.

Although these forces are biologically distinct, the models shared one important property:

they were deterministic.

A deterministic model predicts exactly what happens in the next generation once the initial conditions are known.

If we know:

- genotype fitnesses,
- mutation rates,
- starting allele frequencies,

then we can calculate exactly what allele frequencies will be in the next generation.

For example, under selection we can derive a recursion equation for $p_{t+1}$ and directly compute the evolutionary trajectory.

Nothing random occurs.

If we start two populations with identical conditions, both populations are predicted to evolve identically.

This predictability depends on an assumption that is often hidden within the mathematics:

the population is effectively infinite.

When populations are infinitely large:

- allele frequencies are represented perfectly,
- every generation samples the parental population perfectly,
- no sampling error occurs.

This assumption is mathematically convenient.

Unfortunately, it is biologically unrealistic.

No biological population is infinite.

Even very large populations contain only a finite number of individuals and therefore a finite number of gene copies.

Once populations become finite, evolution becomes a sampling process.

The next generation receives only a sample of the alleles currently present.

Whenever sampling occurs, chance enters the system.

The evolutionary consequences of this finite sampling process are called **genetic drift**.

---

# Revisiting Our Previous Assumptions

Earlier models generally assumed:

- Individuals are diploid.
- We examine one locus.
- Allele frequencies sum to one.
- No additional loci are important.
- No new mutations occur during the modeled process.
- No migration occurs.
- No mate choice occurs.
- No ecological complications alter genotype fitness.
- Populations are effectively infinite.

Today we relax only one assumption:

**Populations are finite.**

Remarkably, that single change introduces an entirely new evolutionary force.

Even if:

- all alleles have identical fitness,
- no mutation occurs,
- no migration occurs,

allele frequencies can still change through time.

The cause is random sampling.

---

# What Is Genetic Drift?

Genetic drift is the random change in allele frequencies caused by finite population size.

Unlike natural selection:

- Drift is not adaptive.
- Drift does not consistently favor one allele over another.
- Drift occurs even when all alleles have identical fitness.

Drift is therefore a stochastic evolutionary process.

The word stochastic means that the outcome depends upon probability.

The exact outcome cannot be predicted in advance, although the distribution of possible outcomes can be described mathematically.

---

# Evolutionary Sampling

To build intuition for drift, consider a familiar statistical problem.

Imagine a giant container containing one million M&Ms.

Suppose the true frequencies are:

- 20% blue,
- 30% orange,
- 50% brown.

Now randomly sample ten M&Ms.

Will the sample contain exactly:

- 2 blue,
- 3 orange,
- 5 brown?

Probably not.

The sample is only an estimate of the population.

Random deviations occur because only a finite sample was collected.

The same principle applies to evolution.

The parental generation contains the true allele frequencies.

The offspring generation represents only a sample drawn from those frequencies.

Consequently:

- offspring frequencies rarely match parental frequencies exactly,
- random deviations occur every generation,
- those deviations are larger when populations are small.

This phenomenon is genetic drift.

---

# Deterministic Versus Stochastic Evolution

Selection is generally deterministic.

If allele $\alpha_i$ has greater fitness than allele $\alpha_j$,

$$
w_i > w_j
$$

then allele $\alpha_i$ is expected to increase in frequency.

The direction of change is predictable.

Drift behaves differently.

Drift is stochastic.

A stochastic process does not produce one predictable outcome.

Instead it produces a distribution of possible outcomes.

For drift:

- We cannot predict exactly what allele frequency will occur next generation.
- We can predict the probability of observing every possible outcome.

The Wright-Fisher model provides precisely this probability distribution.

---

# A Simple Sampling Example

Consider a diploid population containing

$$
N = 10
$$

individuals.

Since individuals are diploid, the population contains

$$
2N = 20
$$

gene copies.

Suppose allele $\alpha_i$ has frequency

$$
p = 0.50
$$

The population therefore contains

$$
20(0.50)=10
$$

copies of allele $\alpha_i$.

Now imagine forming the next generation by randomly sampling twenty gametes.

Must we obtain exactly ten copies of $\alpha_i$?

No.

Possible outcomes include:

| Copies of $\alpha_i$ | Frequency |
|---|---|
| 8 | 0.40 |
| 9 | 0.45 |
| 10 | 0.50 |
| 11 | 0.55 |
| 12 | 0.60 |

Notice that:

- no selection occurred,
- no mutation occurred,
- no migration occurred,

yet allele frequencies changed.

The only cause was finite sampling.

This is genetic drift.

---

# Coin-Flipping Analogy

A useful analogy is coin flipping.

Suppose a fair coin has probability

$$
P(\text{Heads})=0.5
$$

If we flip the coin twenty times, the expected number of heads is

$$
20(0.5)=10
$$

However, we rarely obtain exactly ten heads.

Possible outcomes include:

- 8 heads,
- 11 heads,
- 13 heads.

The expected outcome is known.

The realized outcome varies.

Allele sampling behaves exactly the same way.

Drift is essentially evolutionary sampling error.

---

# Why Population Size Matters

Now consider two samples:

- 10 alleles,
- 10,000 alleles.

Which sample should more accurately represent the parental population?

The larger sample.

This is a fundamental result from statistics.

As sample size increases:

- sampling error decreases,
- estimates become more accurate.

Consequently:

$$
\text{Strength of Drift} \propto \frac{1}{N}
$$

Small populations experience strong drift.

Large populations experience weak drift.

This is one of the most important conclusions of the lecture.

---

# Three Critical Observations from Simulations

Before deriving any equations, simulations reveal three important patterns.

Later we will show mathematically why each pattern occurs.

---

# Observation 1: Smaller Populations Experience More Drift

Define one generation of drift as

$$
\Delta_D p = p_{t+1}-p_t
$$

where:

$$
p_t
$$

is the current allele frequency and

$$
p_{t+1}
$$

is the next-generation allele frequency.

The quantity $\Delta_D p$ measures the amount of evolutionary change generated by one generation of random sampling.

Consider several simulated outcomes:

| Current $p$ | Next $p$ | $\Delta_D p$ |
|---|---|---|
| 0.50 | 0.49 | -0.01 |
| 0.50 | 0.58 | +0.08 |
| 0.50 | 0.46 | -0.04 |
| 0.50 | 0.41 | -0.09 |
| 0.50 | 0.54 | +0.04 |
| 0.50 | 0.50 | 0.00 |

The average change is approximately zero.

However, the magnitude of fluctuations becomes much larger when population size decreases.

Thus small populations drift more strongly.

---

# Observation 2: Extinction Depends on Current Frequency

Simulations reveal that rare alleles frequently disappear.

Common alleles disappear less often.

For example:

An allele beginning at

$$
p=0.01
$$

usually goes extinct.

An allele beginning at

$$
p=0.80
$$

is much more likely to fix.

This observation ultimately leads to one of the most elegant results in population genetics:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=p
$$

The probability of eventual fixation equals the allele's current frequency.

We will derive this result later.

---

# Observation 3: Drift Removes Genetic Variation

Simulations repeatedly show allele frequencies moving toward two special states:

$$
p=0
$$

and

$$
p=1
$$

These represent the absorbing boundaries.

If:

$$
p=0
$$

the allele has been lost.

If:

$$
p=1
$$

the allele has been fixed.

Because no mutation or migration occurs in the Wright-Fisher model, once either boundary is reached there is no mechanism for escape.

Drift therefore destroys genetic variation through time.

---

# The Wright-Fisher Model

The Wright-Fisher model is the simplest mathematical description of genetic drift.

The model was developed independently by:

- Sewall Wright
- Ronald Fisher

The Wright-Fisher model serves as the fundamental null model of population genetics.

Virtually all modern population-genetic theory begins with this framework.

---

# Assumptions of the Wright-Fisher Model

The Wright-Fisher model assumes:

- Diploid population.
- Constant population size.
- Random mating.
- Non-overlapping generations.
- No mutation.
- No migration.
- No selection.
- Random sampling of gametes.

Under these assumptions, drift is the only evolutionary force acting on the population.

---

# Wright-Fisher Reproduction

Let

$$
p_t
$$

denote the frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ in generation $t$.

The parental population contains

$$
2N
$$

gene copies.

The offspring population is produced by sampling

$$
2N
$$

gametes from the parental gamete pool.

Each gamete carries allele $\alpha_i$ with probability

$$
p_t
$$

and allele $\alpha_j$ with probability

$$
1-p_t
$$

Each sampling event is independent.

---

# Deriving the Wright-Fisher Sampling Distribution

Our goal is to determine the probability distribution of allele counts in the next generation.

Let

$$
X
$$

denote the number of copies of allele $\alpha_i$ sampled into generation $t+1$.

Observe the structure of the problem:

- There are $2N$ sampling events.
- Each event produces either success or failure.
- The probability of success is $p_t$.

This is exactly the definition of a binomial random variable.

Therefore:

$$
X \sim \text{Binomial}(2N,p_t)
$$

The probability of observing exactly $k$ copies of allele $\alpha_i$ is

$$
P(X=k)=\binom{2N}{k}p_t^k(1-p_t)^{2N-k}
$$

This is the central equation of the Wright-Fisher model.

Everything else follows from this probability distribution.

---

# Example Calculation

Suppose:

$$
N=5
$$

and

$$
p_t=0.5
$$

Then:

$$
2N=10
$$

gametes are sampled.

What is the probability of obtaining exactly six copies of allele $\alpha_i$?

Using the binomial formula:

$$
P(X=6)=\binom{10}{6}(0.5)^6(0.5)^4
$$

Since

$$
\binom{10}{6}=210
$$

we obtain

$$
P(X=6)=210(0.5)^{10}
$$

Since

$$
(0.5)^{10}=0.0009766
$$

we obtain

$$
P(X=6)\approx0.205
$$

There is therefore approximately a 20.5% chance of obtaining six copies.

---

# Deriving the Expected Allele Frequency

A natural question is:

Does drift systematically push allele frequencies upward or downward?

To answer this question we calculate the expectation.

For a binomial random variable:

$$
E[X]=2Np_t
$$

The next-generation allele frequency is

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{X}{2N}
$$

Now take expectations:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=E\left(\frac{X}{2N}\right)
$$

Because expectation is linear:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=\frac{1}{2N}E[X]
$$

Substituting the binomial expectation:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=\frac{1}{2N}(2Np_t)
$$

Cancelling terms:

$$
E[p_{t+1}]=p_t
$$

This result means:

$$
E[\Delta p]=0
$$

Drift is unbiased.

It changes allele frequencies, but it does not systematically favor any allele.

---

# Deriving the Variance Generated by Drift

Although drift does not change the mean allele frequency, it generates variance.

Variance is what causes populations to diverge from one another.

For a binomial random variable:

$$
\text{Var}(X)=2Np_t(1-p_t)
$$

Since

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{X}{2N}
$$

we have

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\text{Var}\left(\frac{X}{2N}\right)
$$

Using the variance rule

$$
\text{Var}(aX)=a^2\text{Var}(X)
$$

gives

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{1}{4N^2}\text{Var}(X)
$$

Substitute the binomial variance:

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{1}{4N^2}[2Np_t(1-p_t)]
$$

Simplifying:

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N}
$$

This equation quantifies drift strength.

---

# When Is Drift Strongest?

The variance depends on

$$
p(1-p)
$$

Consider:

| $p$ | $p(1-p)$ |
|---|---|
| 0.1 | 0.09 |
| 0.2 | 0.16 |
| 0.5 | 0.25 |
| 0.8 | 0.16 |
| 0.9 | 0.09 |

The maximum occurs when

$$
p=0.5
$$

Therefore drift generates its largest fluctuations at intermediate frequencies.

---

# Genetic Drift as a Random Walk

We have shown:

$$
E[p_{t+1}] = p_t
$$

and

$$
\text{Var}(p_{t+1})=\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N}
$$

The first result tells us drift is unbiased.

The second tells us drift generates random fluctuations.

Together they imply that allele frequencies behave as a random walk.

<br>

```text
Allele Frequency

1.0 |
0.9 |                     *
0.8 |
0.7 |                 *
0.6 |              *
0.5 | *  *  *
0.4 |      *  *
0.3 |
0.2 |
0.1 |
0.0 +------------------------
      Generation
```

<br>

At every generation an allele may:

- increase,
- decrease,
- stay approximately unchanged.

Chance determines the particular trajectory.

---

# Replicate Populations

Suppose multiple populations all begin with:

$$
p_0=0.50
$$

Each population experiences a different sequence of random sampling events.

Consequently:

- each population follows a unique evolutionary path,
- variance among populations increases through time.

Increasing divergence among populations is one of the classic signatures of drift.

---

# Why Fixation Is Inevitable

Allele frequencies are constrained:

$$
0 \le p \le 1
$$

The states

$$
p=0
$$

and

$$
p=1
$$

are absorbing boundaries.

Because drift behaves as a random walk, allele frequencies eventually reach one of these boundaries.

Thus every neutral allele is ultimately:

- lost,
- or fixed.

Drift inevitably removes variation.

---

# Deriving the Probability of Fixation

Let

$$
u
$$

be the probability that a neutral allele eventually fixes.

Eventually every replicate population ends in one of two states:

- fixation ($p=1$),
- loss ($p=0$).

The average final frequency must equal the initial frequency because drift is unbiased.

Therefore:

$$
p=(1)(u)+(0)(1-u)
$$

Simplifying:

$$
u=p
$$

Therefore:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=p
$$

The fixation probability of a neutral allele equals its current frequency.

---

# New Mutations

A new mutation initially appears in a single copy.

Because the population contains

$$
2N
$$

gene copies,

$$
p=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P_{\text{fix}}=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Most new neutral mutations are lost.

Only a very small fraction become fixed.

---

# Measuring Genetic Variation

For a two-allele locus, heterozygosity is

$$
H=2pq
$$

where:

- $p$ is the frequency of $\alpha_i$,
- $q$ is the frequency of $\alpha_j$.

Biologically, heterozygosity represents the probability that two randomly sampled alleles are different.

Our objective is to determine how drift changes $H$ through time.

---

# Identity by State Versus Identity by Descent

Before deriving the loss of heterozygosity we must distinguish two forms of identity.

## Identity by State

Two alleles are identical by state if they possess the same sequence.

Example:

ACCT

ACCT

These are identical by state.

## Identity by Descent

Two alleles are identical by descent if they trace back to a common ancestral copy.

For example:

Parent: ACCT

Offspring: ACCT

These alleles are both:

- identical by state,
- identical by descent.

If mutation later produces ACCG, the alleles may no longer be identical by state but still remain identical by descent.

Identity by descent is the concept that drives the heterozygosity derivation.

---

# Defining Homozygosity

Directly calculating heterozygosity is difficult.

Instead it is easier to calculate its complement.

Let

$$
Q_t
$$

denote the probability that two sampled alleles are identical.

This quantity is homozygosity.

Because alleles must be either:

- identical,
- different,

we have

$$
Q_t + H_t = 1
$$

Therefore:

$$
Q_t = 1-H_t
$$

and

$$
H_t = 1-Q_t
$$

Our strategy will be:

1. Derive a recursion for $Q$.
2. Convert the recursion into a formula for $H$.

---

# Step 1: Sample Two Alleles

Imagine sampling two alleles from generation $t+1$.

We ask:

"What is the probability that these two alleles are identical?"

There are two mutually exclusive possibilities.

---

# Route 1: Both Alleles Descend from the Same Parental Copy

The first offspring allele chooses a parental gene copy.

There are

$$
2N
$$

possible parental copies.

For the second offspring allele to choose that exact same copy:

$$
P(\text{same parent})=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

If this occurs, the two offspring alleles are automatically identical.

Thus this route contributes

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

to homozygosity.

---

# Route 2: Different Parental Copies

The probability that the second offspring chooses a different parental copy is

$$
1-\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Now identity is not guaranteed.

The offspring alleles will be identical only if the parental copies were already identical.

The probability of that event is

$$
Q_t
$$

Applying the multiplication rule:

$$
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)Q_t
$$

---

# Step 2: Add Both Routes

The total probability of homozygosity is therefore

$$
Q_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)Q_t
$$

This recursion describes how drift increases identity-by-descent through time.

Notice the biological interpretation.

Drift does not create homozygosity because it prefers particular alleles.

Instead repeated sampling continually traces lineages back to fewer and fewer ancestral copies.

As ancestral diversity disappears, homozygosity increases.

---

# Step 3: Convert Homozygosity Into Heterozygosity

Substitute

$$
Q_t = 1-H_t
$$

and

$$
Q_{t+1}=1-H_{t+1}
$$

into the recursion.

This gives

$$
1-H_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)(1-H_t)
$$

Expand the right side:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=\frac{1}{2N}+1-\frac{1}{2N}-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Cancel terms:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=1-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Subtract one from both sides:

$$
-H_{t+1}=-H_t+\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Multiply by $-1$:

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t-\frac{H_t}{2N}
$$

Factor out $H_t$:

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

This is one of the most important equations in population genetics.

---

# Interpreting the Heterozygosity Recursion

Each generation removes the fraction

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

of the heterozygosity that remains.

Therefore:

- Small populations lose variation rapidly.
- Large populations lose variation slowly.

This result mathematically explains why drift drives populations toward fixation and extinction.

---

# Heterozygosity Through Time

Apply the recursion repeatedly.

After one generation:

$$
H_1=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

After two generations:

$$
H_2=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^2
$$

After $t$ generations:

$$
H_t=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^t
$$

This equation describes the gradual loss of neutral genetic variation through time.

---

# Consequences of Genetic Drift

Genetic drift causes:

- Random allele-frequency changes.
- Divergence among populations.
- Allele fixation.
- Allele loss.
- Increased homozygosity.
- Reduced heterozygosity.
- Loss of genetic variation.
- Increased population differentiation.

These effects are strongest when populations are small.

---

# Big Picture

The Wright-Fisher model describes evolution as a sampling process in finite populations.

Under this model:

- Alleles are randomly sampled between generations.
- Drift changes allele frequencies by chance.
- Expected allele frequencies remain unchanged.
- Variance accumulates through time.
- Allele frequencies follow random walks.
- Neutral alleles eventually become fixed or lost.
- Homozygosity increases through time.
- Heterozygosity declines through time.
- Genetic variation is gradually eliminated.

The Wright-Fisher model forms the foundation for:

- Effective population size,
- Inbreeding theory,
- Coalescent theory,
- Neutral theory,
- Molecular evolution,
- Population genomics.

Virtually all modern population genetics begins with the Wright-Fisher framework.

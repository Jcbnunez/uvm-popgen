# Introduction: A Problem Created by Drift

In the previous lecture we developed the Wright-Fisher model and derived one of the most important results in population genetics:

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

This equation showed that genetic drift continuously removes heterozygosity from finite populations.

The biological interpretation was straightforward:

- Small populations lose variation rapidly.
- Large populations lose variation slowly.
- Given enough time, drift eventually eliminates variation altogether.

This result creates an important puzzle.

If drift continually removes genetic variation, why do natural populations remain genetically variable?

Consider:

- Humans contain millions of segregating genetic variants.
- Drosophila populations contain enormous genetic variation.
- Many marine organisms possess extraordinary levels of polymorphism.

If drift continuously destroys variation, where does that variation come from?

The answer is mutation.

Mutation creates variation.

Drift removes variation.

The genetic diversity we observe in natural populations reflects a balance between these two opposing evolutionary forces.

The first half of this lecture explores that balance.

The second half explains why the census population size often fails to predict the strength of drift and introduces the concept of effective population size.

---

# Mutation and Drift as Opposing Forces

Recall that heterozygosity is

$$
H=2pq
$$

where:

- $p$ is the frequency of allele $\alpha_i$
- $q$ is the frequency of allele $\alpha_j$
- $p+q=1$

Biologically, heterozygosity is the probability that two randomly sampled alleles differ.

Last lecture we showed that drift changes heterozygosity by

$$
\Delta H_{\text{drift}}=
-\frac{H}{2N}
$$

The negative sign tells us that drift removes variation.

Mutation acts in the opposite direction.

Whenever a mutation occurs, a new genetic state is generated.

Mutation therefore increases genetic diversity and increases heterozygosity.

Consequently evolution can be viewed as a continual tug-of-war:

<br>

```text
Mutation ------------------> Creates variation

Drift    <------------------ Removes variation
```

<br>

The key question becomes:

How do these two forces interact when both occur simultaneously?

---

# Using Homozygosity to Model Mutation and Drift

In the previous lecture we defined

$$
Q_t
$$

as the probability that two randomly sampled alleles are identical.

Thus:

$$
Q_t+H_t=1
$$

and therefore

$$
Q_t=1-H_t
$$

We will begin by deriving the dynamics of $Q$ and then convert the result back into heterozygosity.

This strategy simplifies the mathematics because identity is easier to track than difference.

---

# The Logic of the Mutation-Drift Model

Suppose we sample two alleles from generation $t+1$.

For those alleles to be identical, two independent conditions must both be true:

1. Drift must produce identity-by-descent.
2. Mutation must fail to disrupt that identity.

Thus,

$$
Q_{t+1}=
Q_{N,t+1}
Q_{\mu,t+1}
$$

where:

- $Q_{N,t+1}$ represents the effect of drift.
- $Q_{\mu,t+1}$ represents the effect of mutation.

The multiplication appears because both events must occur simultaneously.

---

# The Mutation Component

Suppose the mutation rate per chromosome is

$$
\mu
$$

For two alleles to remain identical, neither chromosome can mutate.

The probability that one chromosome avoids mutation is

$$
1-\mu
$$

The probability that both avoid mutation is

$$
Q_{\mu,t+1}=
(1-\mu)^2
$$

Expanding:

$$
Q_{\mu,t+1}=
1-2\mu+\mu^2
$$

Mutation rates are typically extremely small.

For example:

$$
\mu=10^{-5}
$$

or

$$
\mu=10^{-8}
$$

Consequently

$$
\mu^2
$$

is vanishingly small.

We therefore use the approximation

$$
Q_{\mu,t+1}
\approx
1-2\mu
$$

This approximation is used throughout population genetics.

---

# The Drift Component

From the previous lecture we derived

$$
Q_{N,t+1}=
\frac{1}{2N}
+
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)Q_t
$$

Recall the interpretation.

The first term represents the probability that both offspring alleles descend from the same parental copy.

The second term represents the probability that they descend from different parental copies that were already identical.

---

# Combining Mutation and Drift

Substituting both components gives

$$
Q_{t+1}=
(1-2\mu)
\left[
\frac{1}{2N}
+
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)Q_t
\right]
$$

Distributing terms:

$$
Q_{t+1}=
\frac{1-2\mu}{2N}
+
(1-2\mu)
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)
Q_t
$$

Expanding:

$$
Q_{t+1}=
\frac{1}{2N}+
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)Q_t+
\frac{2\mu}{2N}-
2\mu Q_t-
\frac{2\mu Q_t}{2N}
$$

Now notice something important.

Both

$$
\mu
$$

and

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

are already small quantities.

Their product is therefore extremely small.

Consequently we neglect the terms

$$
\frac{2\mu}{2N}
\approx 0
$$

and

$$
\frac{2\mu Q_t}{2N}
\approx 0
$$

leaving

$$
Q_{t+1}=
\frac{1}{2N}+
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)Q_t-
2\mu Q_t
$$

This equation describes the simultaneous action of mutation and drift.

---

# Converting Back to Heterozygosity

Recall:

$$
Q_t=1-H_t
$$

and

$$
Q_{t+1}=1-H_{t+1}
$$

Substituting into the recursion:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=
\frac{1}{2N}
+
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N}
\right)
(1-H_t)-
2\mu(1-H_t)
$$

Expanding:

$$
1-H_{t+1}=
\frac{1}{2N}+1-
\frac{1}{2N}-
H_t
+\frac{H_t}{2N}
-2\mu(1-H_t)
$$

Cancel terms:

$$
-H_{t+1}=
-H_t
+\frac{H_t}{2N}-
2\mu(1-H_t)
$$

Add $H_t$ to both sides:

$$
H_{t+1}-H_t=
-\frac{H_t}{2N}
+
2\mu(1-H_t)
$$

Recognizing the left side as a finite difference:

$$
\Delta H=
-\frac{H}{2N}
+
2\mu(1-H)
$$

This is the fundamental mutation-drift equation.

---

# Interpreting the Mutation-Drift Equation

The equation

$$
\Delta H=
-\frac{H}{2N}
+
2\mu(1-H)
$$

contains two competing forces.

The first term

$$
-\frac{H}{2N}
$$

represents drift.

Drift decreases heterozygosity.

The second term

$$
2\mu(1-H)
$$

represents mutation.

Mutation increases heterozygosity.

Thus

$$
\Delta H=
\Delta_{\text{drift}}H
+\Delta_{\text{mutation}}H
$$

The future genetic diversity of a population depends on which force is stronger.

---

# Mutation-Drift Equilibrium

At equilibrium, heterozygosity is no longer changing.

Therefore:

$$
\Delta H=0
$$

Substituting into the mutation-drift equation:

$$
0=
-\frac{H}{2N}+
2\mu(1-H)
$$

Move the drift term:

$$
\frac{H}{2N}=
2\mu(1-H)
$$

Multiply by $2N$:

$$
H=
4N\mu(1-H)
$$

Expand:

$$
H=
4N\mu-
4N\mu H
$$

Bring all $H$ terms together:

$$
H+4N\mu H=
4N\mu
$$

Factor:

$$
H(1+4N\mu)=
4N\mu
$$

Solving for $H$:

$$
H=
\frac{4N\mu}{1+4N\mu}
$$

This is the expected equilibrium heterozygosity under mutation-drift balance.

---

# Interpreting the Equilibrium

The equilibrium heterozygosity depends entirely on:

- population size ($N$)
- mutation rate ($\mu$)

Consider two limiting cases.

## Small Populations

If

$$
4N\mu \ll 1
$$

then

$$
H \approx 4N\mu
$$

Drift overwhelms mutation.

Variation remains low.

## Large Populations

If

$$
4N\mu \gg 1
$$

then

$$
H \approx 1
$$

Mutation replenishes variation faster than drift removes it.

Variation remains high.

At first glance this appears to solve the problem of predicting genetic diversity.

Unfortunately, empirical data revealed another puzzle.

---

# Why Census Population Size Often Fails

Population geneticists soon noticed that observed populations frequently lose variation more rapidly than predicted by census size.

In many experiments:

- heterozygosity decays too quickly,
- drift appears too strong,
- observed variation is lower than expected.

This suggests that census size may not be the quantity evolution actually experiences.

In other words, a population containing 1,000 individuals may not genetically behave like a population of 1,000 individuals.

This realization led to one of the most important concepts in evolutionary biology:

effective population size.

---

# Census Population Size Versus Effective Population Size

The census size is

$$
N
$$

which is simply the total number of individuals present.

However, not all individuals contribute equally to future generations.

Some individuals:

- leave no offspring,
- contribute very few gametes,
- fail to reproduce entirely.

Drift is determined by the individuals that actually contribute genes to the future.

This motivates the concept of effective population size.

---

# Defining Effective Population Size

Effective population size, denoted

$$
N_e
$$

is the size of an ideal Wright-Fisher population that experiences the same amount of drift as the real population.

This definition is subtle but important.

$N_e$ is not necessarily the number of breeding individuals.

Instead it is the size of an idealized population that would show the same evolutionary behavior.

For most species:

$$
N_e < N
$$

sometimes dramatically so.

---

# Unequal Sex Ratios

One important factor reducing $N_e$ is unequal contribution by males and females.

Suppose a population contains:

- many breeding females,
- very few breeding males.

Although many individuals exist, relatively few lineages contribute genes to the next generation.

Drift therefore becomes stronger.

The effective population size is

$$
N_e=
\frac{4N_fN_m}{N_f+N_m}
$$

where:

- $N_f$ = breeding females
- $N_m$ = breeding males

---

# Example: Extreme Sex Imbalance

Suppose

$$
N_f=100
$$

and

$$
N_m=1
$$

Then

$$
N_e=
\frac{4(100)(1)}{101}
$$

which gives

$$
N_e\approx3.96
$$

Remarkably, a population containing 101 breeding adults genetically behaves as though only about 4 individuals were reproducing.

This explains why species with highly skewed reproductive success may exhibit unexpectedly strong drift.

---

# Long-Term Effective Population Size

Most populations fluctuate through time.

They experience:

- expansions,
- contractions,
- bottlenecks,
- recoveries.

How should we calculate average effective population size through time?

The answer is not the arithmetic mean.

Instead, evolution is governed by the harmonic mean.

---

# Harmonic Mean Effective Population Size

Suppose effective population size equals

| Generation | $N_e$ |
|------------|---------|
| t | 100 |
| t+1 | 100 |
| t+2 | 20 |
| t+3 | 100 |

The arithmetic mean is

$$
\frac{100+100+20+100}{4}=
80
$$

However this is not evolutionarily meaningful.

The correct long-term effective population size is

$$
N_e=
\frac{T}
{
\frac{1}{N_{e1}}
+\frac{1}{N_{e2}}
+\cdots+
\frac{1}{N_{eT}}
}
$$

Substituting the values:

$$
N_e=
\frac{4}
{
\frac{1}{100}
+\frac{1}{100}
+\frac{1}{20}
+\frac{1}{100}
}
$$

which gives

$$
N_e=50
$$

Notice that a single bottleneck generation dramatically reduced long-term effective size.

---

# Why Bottlenecks Matter So Much

This result illustrates one of the most important principles in conservation genetics.

Population contractions matter far more than population expansions.

A population may spend decades at large size.

Yet a brief bottleneck can dramatically reduce long-term effective population size.

Evolution remembers bottlenecks because drift is strongest when populations are small.

Consequently:

- endangered populations are vulnerable to rapid loss of diversity,
- historical bottlenecks leave long-lasting genetic signatures,
- conservation biologists often focus on maintaining large effective population sizes.

---

# Rewriting Population Genetic Equations Using Effective Population Size

Because drift is governed by effective population size, most population genetic equations replace $N$ with $N_e$.

The mutation-drift equation becomes

$$
\Delta H=
-\frac{H}{2N_e}
+
2\mu(1-H)
$$

The equilibrium heterozygosity becomes

$$
H=
\frac{4N_e\mu}
{1+4N_e\mu}
$$

These are the equations most commonly used in modern population genetics.

---

# Big Picture

In the previous lecture we learned that drift removes variation.

In this lecture we discovered that mutation continually replenishes variation.

Their interaction produces mutation-drift balance:

$$
H=
\frac{4N_e\mu}
{1+4N_e\mu}
$$

The key parameter is not census population size.

It is effective population size.

Effective population size determines:

- the strength of drift,
- the maintenance of variation,
- the severity of bottlenecks,
- the long-term evolutionary potential of populations.

For this reason, $N_e$ is one of the most important parameters in all of evolutionary genetics.

The remainder of the course will repeatedly demonstrate that understanding evolution often boils down to understanding one quantity:

$$
N_e
$$

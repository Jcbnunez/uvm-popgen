# Introduction: A Change in Perspective

Up to this point, nearly every model we have studied has been a **forward-time model**.

In a forward-time model we begin with a current population and ask:

> What happens in the next generation?

Examples include:

- Selection
- Mutation
- Genetic drift
- Mutation-drift balance

All of these models ask how allele frequencies change as generations move forward.

Coalescent theory asks a fundamentally different question.

Instead of asking:

> What will happen next generation?

we ask:

> Where did today's alleles come from?

Rather than moving forward through time, we move backward through time.

Instead of tracking allele frequencies, we track ancestry.

Instead of studying populations, we study genealogies.

Coalescent theory is therefore the mathematical study of ancestry.

---

# Why Do We Need a New Theory?

Recall the Wright-Fisher model.

Suppose a population contains

$$
N = 1000
$$

diploid individuals.

The population therefore contains

$$
2N = 2000
$$

gene copies.

Tracking all 2000 gene copies forward through time becomes extremely complicated.

Suppose instead that we sample only two alleles today.

Rather than following the entire population, we can ask:

> How long ago did these two alleles share a common ancestor?

This turns out to be much easier.

Coalescent theory exploits this simplicity.

Rather than tracking everybody forward through time, it tracks only the ancestral lineages that matter.

---

# What Is a Coalescent Event?

Consider two sampled alleles:

$$
\alpha_i
$$

and

$$
\alpha_j
$$

from the present generation.

Moving backward through time:

- Each allele traces to a parental copy.
- Those parental copies trace to grandparents.
- Grandparental copies trace further into the past.

Eventually the two lineages merge into a single ancestral lineage.

That merging event is called a **coalescent event**.

The ancestor at which the lineages merge is called a **common ancestor**.

The most recent ancestor shared by all sampled alleles is called the

**Most Recent Common Ancestor (MRCA).**

# Coalescence Under Wright-Fisher Sampling

We now derive the probability of coalescence.

Assume a standard Wright-Fisher population:

- Diploid
- Constant size
- Random mating
- No selection
- No migration
- No mutation

Suppose we sample two alleles from generation $t$.

Moving backward one generation, each allele must choose one parental copy from

$$
2N
$$

possible parental copies.

We ask:

What is the probability that both sampled alleles choose the same parent?

If the first allele chooses a parental copy, there are still

$$
2N
$$

possible parental copies for the second allele.

Only one of those choices matches the first allele's ancestor.

Therefore

$$
P(\text{coalescence in previous generation})=
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

This is the fundamental probability in coalescent theory.

---

# Probability of No Coalescence

If coalescence occurs with probability

$$
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

then no coalescence occurs with probability

$$
1-\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Biologically this means the two sampled lineages remain distinct one generation further into the past.

---

# Waiting Time to Coalescence

Let

$$
T_2
$$

denote the number of generations required for two sampled alleles to coalesce.

The probability that coalescence occurs exactly one generation in the past is

$$
P(T_2=1)=
\frac{1}{2N}
$$

The probability that coalescence occurs exactly two generations in the past is

$$
P(T_2=2)=
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

The probability that coalescence occurs exactly three generations in the past is

$$
P(T_2=3)=
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^2
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

Continuing:

$$
P(T_2=t)=
\left(1-\frac{1}{2N}\right)^{t-1}
\left(\frac{1}{2N}\right)
$$

This is a geometric distribution.

---

# Expected Waiting Time for Two Lineages

For a geometric distribution with success probability

$$
p
$$

the expected waiting time is

$$
E[T]=
\frac{1}{p}
$$

In our case

$$
p=\frac{1}{2N}
$$

Therefore

$$
E[T_2]=
\frac{1}{1/(2N)}
$$

which simplifies to

$$
E[T_2]=
2N
$$

generations.

This is one of the most important results in coalescent theory.

---

# Biological Interpretation

The expected coalescence time for two alleles equals

$$
2N
$$

generations.

Notice immediately:

- Small populations coalesce rapidly.
- Large populations coalesce slowly.

For example:

| Population Size | Expected Coalescence Time |
|---|---|
| 100 | 200 generations |
| 1,000 | 2,000 generations |
| 10,000 | 20,000 generations |

Thus large populations preserve ancestral lineages much longer than small populations.

---

# Effective Population Size

Recall from the previous lecture that drift is governed by effective population size:

$$
N_e
$$

rather than census size.

Therefore the coalescent result becomes

$$
E[T_2]=
2N_e
$$

This version is used almost universally in population genetics.

---

# Probability That Two Alleles Have Not Yet Coalesced

Suppose we ask:

What is the probability that two alleles remain distinct after $t$ generations?

They must avoid coalescence every generation.

The probability of avoiding coalescence in one generation is

$$
1-\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(\text{No Coalescence by generation } t)=
\left(
1-\frac{1}{2N_e}
\right)^t
$$

This quantity is often called the survival probability of two lineages.

---

# Connecting Coalescent Theory to Heterozygosity

Recall from the previous lecture that heterozygosity depends on the probability that two alleles differ.

Now imagine following two alleles backward through time.

If they coalesce recently, there has been little time for mutations to accumulate. The alleles will probably be identical. If they coalesce far in the past, there has been much more time for mutations to accumulate. The alleles will probably differ. Thus heterozygosity is fundamentally determined by coalescence times.

This is one of the deepest insights in modern population genetics.

---

# Extending the Theory to Three Lineages

Suppose we sample three alleles.

At the present:

<br>

```text
A
B
C
```

<br>

Moving backward, the first coalescent event reduces three lineages to two.

A second coalescent event then reduces two lineages to one.

Therefore the genealogy contains two separate waiting periods.

To model this process we must calculate the probability that any pair coalesces.

---

# Number of Possible Pairs

With

$$
k
$$

lineages, the number of possible pairs equals

$$
\binom{k}{2}=
\frac{k(k-1)}{2}
$$

This quantity arises repeatedly in coalescent theory.

For example:

| Number of Lineages | Possible Pairs |
|---|---|
| 2 | 1 |
| 3 | 3 |
| 4 | 6 |
| 5 | 10 |

---

# Coalescence Rate for k Lineages

Each pair coalesces with probability

$$
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

per generation.

Because there are

$$
\binom{k}{2}
$$

possible pairs, the probability of observing a coalescent event in the previous generation is

$$
\lambda_k=
\frac{\binom{k}{2}}{2N_e}
$$

Substituting the pair count:

$$
\lambda_k=
\frac{k(k-1)}{4N_e}
$$

This is the coalescent rate when there are $k$ lineages.

---

# Expected Waiting Time Between Coalescent Events

The expected waiting time equals the reciprocal of the rate.

Therefore

$$
E[T_k]=
\frac{1}{\lambda_k}
$$

Substituting:

$$
E[T_k]=
\frac{4N_e}{k(k-1)}
$$

This result describes the expected waiting time until the next coalescent event.

---

# Checking Special Cases

## Two Lineages

Substitute

$$
k=2
$$

$$
E[T_2]=
\frac{4N_e}{2(1)}
$$

Therefore

$$
E[T_2]=
2N_e
$$

which matches our earlier result.

---

## Three Lineages

Substitute

$$
k=3
$$

$$
E[T_3]=
\frac{4N_e}{3(2)}
$$

Therefore

$$
E[T_3]=
\frac{2N_e}{3}
$$

Notice that coalescence occurs faster when more lineages are present.

This makes intuitive sense because more pairs are available to merge.

---

# Time to the Most Recent Common Ancestor

Suppose we sample

$$
n
$$

alleles.

To obtain their MRCA we must successively reduce

$$
n
\rightarrow
n-1
\rightarrow
n-2
\rightarrow
\cdots
\rightarrow
1
$$

lineages.

Therefore the expected time to the MRCA equals the sum of all waiting times:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
E[T_n]
+
E[T_{n-1}]
+
\cdots
+
E[T_2]
$$

Substituting our waiting-time formula:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
\sum_{k=2}^{n}
\frac{4N_e}{k(k-1)}
$$

Factor out the constant:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
4N_e
\sum_{k=2}^{n}
\frac{1}{k(k-1)}
$$

---

# Simplifying the Series

Notice that

$$
\frac{1}{k(k-1)}=
\frac{1}{k-1}-
\frac{1}{k}
$$

Using this identity:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
4N_e
\sum_{k=2}^{n}
\left(
\frac{1}{k-1}-
\frac{1}{k}
\right)
$$

Expanding:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
4N_e
\left[
\left(1-\frac{1}{2}\right)
+
\left(\frac{1}{2}-\frac{1}{3}\right)
+
\left(\frac{1}{3}-\frac{1}{4}\right)
+\cdots+
\left(\frac{1}{n-1}-\frac{1}{n}\right)
\right]
$$

All intermediate terms cancel.

Only the endpoints remain:

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
4N_e
\left(
1-\frac{1}{n}
\right)
$$

This remarkable result gives the expected age of the MRCA for a sample of size $n$.

---

# Large Sample Limit

As sample size becomes very large,

$$
n \rightarrow \infty
$$

the term

$$
\frac{1}{n}
\rightarrow 0
$$

Therefore

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]
\rightarrow
4N_e
$$

An important consequence follows.

Even enormous samples do not greatly increase the age of the MRCA.

The age approaches approximately

$$
4N_e
$$

generations.

---

# Total Branch Length

Another quantity of interest is the total amount of genealogy represented by all branches.

When there are

$$
k
$$

lineages present, there are

$$
k
$$

simultaneous branches.

The expected contribution to genealogy during that interval is

$$
kE[T_k]
$$

Substituting:

$$
kE[T_k]=
k
\left(
\frac{4N_e}{k(k-1)}
\right)
$$

which simplifies to

$$
kE[T_k]=
\frac{4N_e}{k-1}
$$

Therefore total branch length equals

$$
L=
\sum_{k=2}^{n}
\frac{4N_e}{k-1}
$$

or

$$
L=
4N_e
\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}
\frac{1}{k}
$$

This quantity plays a central role in molecular evolution and DNA sequence analysis.

---

# Mutations on Genealogies

The coalescent itself describes ancestry.

Mutations are then added onto the genealogy.

Suppose mutations occur at rate

$$
\mu
$$

per generation.

A branch with length

$$
t
$$

generations accumulates an expected

$$
\mu t
$$

mutations.

A genealogy with larger branch lengths accumulates more mutations.

Consequently:

- Large populations tend to contain more sequence diversity.
- Small populations tend to contain less sequence diversity.

The reason is not that mutation rates differ.

The reason is that lineages persist longer before coalescing.

---

# The Coalescent Interpretation of Genetic Diversity

We can now reinterpret everything we previously learned.

Drift removes variation because drift causes lineages to coalesce.

Small populations:

- coalesce quickly,
- possess short genealogies,
- contain little variation.

Large populations:

- coalesce slowly,
- possess deep genealogies,
- contain greater variation.

Thus coalescent theory and drift theory describe the same process from different perspectives.

Forward-time drift describes how allele frequencies change.

Backward-time coalescence describes why those changes occur.

---

# Big Picture

Coalescent theory is the backward-time counterpart of the Wright-Fisher model.

The central results are:

$$
P(\text{Coalescence in One Generation})=
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

$$
E[T_2]=
2N_e
$$

$$
E[T_k]=
\frac{4N_e}{k(k-1)}
$$

$$
E[T_{MRCA}]=
4N_e
\left(
1-\frac{1}{n}
\right)
$$

These equations reveal that:

- Effective population size determines genealogical depth.
- Genetic diversity reflects the age of ancestral lineages.
- Drift and coalescence are two views of the same evolutionary process.
- Modern population genomics is fundamentally built upon coalescent theory.

The Wright-Fisher model asks:

> How do alleles change through time?

Coalescent theory asks:

> Where did today's alleles come from?

Together these perspectives form the mathematical foundation of modern population genetics.

# 1. What is Demographic History?

Demographic history describes how the size, structure, and movement of populations have changed through time.

Important demographic processes include:

- Population growth
- Population decline
- Bottlenecks
- Founder events
- Population subdivision
- Migration
- Admixture
- Population expansion after colonization

These processes alter genetic diversity through their effects on:

- Genetic drift
- Coalescence rates
- Allele frequencies
- Linkage disequilibrium

The central goal is:

> Given modern genomic data, infer the historical demographic events that produced it.

---

# 2. Effective Population Size ($N_e$)

The most important demographic parameter is the effective population size.

Recall:

- Census population size = actual number of individuals ($N$)
- Effective population size = idealized population giving the same amount of drift ($N_e$)

Demography influences genetic variation primarily through $N_e$.

---

## Genetic Drift Variance

Let

- $p_t$ = frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ in generation $t$
- Diploid population size = $N_e$

There are $2N_e$ gene copies.

The number of copies in the next generation follows:

$$
X \sim Binomial(2N_e,p_t)
$$

The next generation allele frequency is

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{X}{2N_e}
$$

With Variance:

$$
Var(p_{t+1})=\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N_e}
$$

### Biological Interpretation

Smaller populations experience:

- Larger variance in allele frequencies
- Faster drift
- Faster loss of diversity

Thus many demographic inference methods attempt to estimate historical changes in $N_e$.

---

# 3. Genetic Diversity as a Record of Demography

Genomes contain information about demographic history because demographic processes alter genealogies.

Consider two populations:

Population A:
- Constant size

Population B:
- Severe bottleneck

Even if both have the same current size, their genomes differ because their ancestral genealogies differ.

The genealogy is the historical record that generates mutations.

Demographic inference therefore often proceeds:

Population History
→ Genealogy
→ Mutations
→ Genome Data

---

# 4. Coalescent Theory as a Framework

Coalescent theory reconstructs ancestry backward in time.

Instead of asking:

"How do alleles move forward?"

we ask:

"How long ago did sampled alleles share a common ancestor?"

---

## Probability of Coalescence

Suppose we sample two alleles:

- $\alpha_i$
- $\alpha_j$

from a diploid population of size $N_e$.

Moving backward one generation:

Each allele chooses one parent among $2N_e$ ancestral gene copies.

The probability they share the same parent is

$$
P(coalescence)=\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

Therefore:

$$
P(no\ coalescence)=1-\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

The probability that coalescence occurs exactly $t$ generations ago is

$$
P(T=t)=\left(1-\frac{1}{2N_e}\right)^{t-1}\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

This is a geometric distribution.

---

## Expected Coalescent Time

For a geometric distribution:

$$
E[T]=\frac{1}{1/(2N_e)}
$$

Therefore:

$$
E[T]=2N_e
$$

### Interpretation

Larger populations:

- Have deeper genealogies
- Maintain diversity longer

Smaller populations:

- Coalesce more rapidly
- Lose diversity faster

This result forms the foundation of demographic inference.

---

# 5. Population Bottlenecks

A bottleneck is a temporary reduction in population size.

Example:

Current history:

10000 → 200 → 10000

The middle reduction greatly accelerates coalescence.

---

## Why Bottlenecks Reduce Diversity

Expected heterozygosity is

$$
H_t=2p_t(1-p_t)
$$

Drift reduces heterozygosity each generation by

$$
H_{t+1}=H_t\left(1-\frac{1}{2N_e}\right)
$$

Applying recursively for $t$ generations:

$$
H_t=H_0\left(1-\frac{1}{2N_e}\right)^t
$$

### Special Case

During a bottleneck:

$$
N_e \downarrow
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{1}{2N_e}\uparrow
$$

and diversity declines rapidly.

---

## Genomic Signature of Bottlenecks

Expected observations:

- Reduced heterozygosity
- Reduced nucleotide diversity
- Excess linkage disequilibrium
- Excess intermediate-frequency alleles
- Long genomic tracts of identity-by-descent

---

# 6. Population Expansion

Now consider rapid population growth.

A population expands:

1000 → 100000

looking forward.

Backward in time this means most lineages rapidly collapse into a smaller ancestral population.

The genealogy has:

- Long internal branches
- Many recent tips

Mutations occurring on terminal branches are typically rare.

Therefore expansion leads to:

- Excess rare alleles
- Many singletons
- Negative Tajima's D

---

# 7. Site Frequency Spectrum (SFS)

The Site Frequency Spectrum is one of the most important demographic summaries.

---

## Definition

Suppose we sample $n$ chromosomes.

At a polymorphic site, count the number carrying the derived allele.

For every derived allele count, record:

- singleton = frequency 1
- doubleton = frequency 2
- etc.

The resulting vector is the SFS.

Example:

| Derived Count | Sites |
|--------------|--------|
|1|500|
|2|230|
|3|160|
|4|120|

---

# Why Does Demography Affect the SFS?

Every mutation appears on a branch of the genealogy.

Rare variants occur on terminal branches.

Intermediate-frequency variants occur on deeper branches.

Because demographic events modify branch lengths:

Demography changes the SFS.

---

## Expansion Signature

Population growth creates:

- many terminal branches

Result:

large excess of singletons.

---

## Bottleneck Signature

Bottlenecks shorten genealogies.

Result:

- loss of rare alleles
- altered intermediate-frequency classes

The SFS becomes a highly informative demographic fingerprint.

---

# 8. Tajima's D

Tajima's D compares two estimators of population genetic diversity.

---

## Estimator 1: Nucleotide Diversity

Nucleotide diversity:

$$
\pi=\text{average pairwise differences}
$$

---

## Estimator 2: Watterson's Estimator

Let

$$
S = \text{segregating sites}
$$

Then

$$
\theta_W=\frac{S}{a_n}
$$

where

$$
a_n=\sum_{k=1}^{n-1}\frac{1}{k}
$$

---

## Tajima's D

Conceptually:

$$
D \propto \pi-\theta_W
$$

---

### Interpretation

Negative $D$

- excess rare variants
- population growth

Positive $D$

- deficiency of rare variants
- bottleneck
- balancing selection

Approximately zero

- constant population size

---

# 9. Linkage Disequilibrium and Demography

Demographic events affect correlations among loci.

Define linkage disequilibrium as

$$
D=P(\alpha_i\beta_i)-p_{\alpha_i}p_{\beta_i}
$$

where:

- $P(\alpha_i\beta_i)$ = haplotype frequency
- $p_{\alpha_i}$ = allele frequency
- $p_{\beta_i}$ = allele frequency

---

## Bottleneck Effects

After a bottleneck:

- fewer ancestral chromosomes survive
- chromosome segments become correlated

Therefore:

$$
LD \uparrow
$$

Many demographic methods use LD decay to reconstruct historical population size.

---

# 10. Inferring Population Size Through Time

Modern methods infer a trajectory

$$
N_e(t)
$$

where effective population size changes through time.

Goal:

Estimate

$$
N_e
$$

for many historical periods.

---

## Conceptual Logic

If coalescent events occur:

- recently → small recent population

If coalescent events occur:

- far in the past → large population

Thus genealogy shape reveals historical size.

---

# 11. PSMC and MSMC

Important genome-scale methods include:

### PSMC
Pairwise Sequentially Markovian Coalescent

Uses:

- one diploid genome

Estimates:

$$
N_e(t)
$$

across time.

---

### MSMC
Multiple Sequentially Markovian Coalescent

Uses:

- multiple genomes

Provides:

- improved temporal resolution
- estimates of population separation

---

## Core Idea

Across chromosomes:

different genomic regions possess different genealogies.

Regions with:

- recent coalescence imply smaller ancestral populations

Regions with:

- ancient coalescence imply larger ancestral populations

The method combines information across the genome.

---

# 12. SFS-Based Demographic Inference

Programs including:

- dadi
- fastsimcoal2
- moments

compare observed SFS to predicted SFS.

Procedure:

1. Propose demographic model
2. Simulate expected SFS
3. Compare with observed SFS
4. Adjust parameters
5. Maximize likelihood

Parameters estimated:

- ancestral $N_e$
- bottleneck severity
- migration rates
- split times
- growth rates

---

# 13. Admixture and Migration

Populations rarely remain isolated.

Suppose populations A and B exchange migrants.

If migration rate is $m$:

$$
p_A'=(1-m)p_A+mp_B
$$

### Interpretation

A fraction $m$ of alleles in population A originates from population B.

Migration generates:

- shared alleles
- reduced divergence
- mixed ancestry tracts

These signatures permit estimation of historical gene flow.

---

# 14. Identity by Descent (IBD)

Two chromosome segments are IBD if inherited from the same ancestral chromosome.

Long IBD segments:

- recent common ancestors

Short IBD segments:

- ancient common ancestors

Therefore distributions of IBD tract lengths contain demographic information.

---

# Summary of Demographic Signatures

| Event | Diversity | Rare Variants | LD | Coalescent Times |
|---------|---------|---------|---------|---------|
| Expansion | Moderate/High | Increase | Decrease | Recent tips, deep roots |
| Bottleneck | Decrease | Decrease | Increase | Rapid coalescence |
| Founder Event | Decrease | Decrease | Increase | Shallow genealogy |
| Migration | Increase | Mixed | Variable | Shared ancestry |
| Constant Size | Stable | Neutral expectation | Moderate | Predictable |

---

# Grand Synthesis

The central principle of demographic inference is:

$$
Demographic\ History
\rightarrow
Genealogies
\rightarrow
Mutations
\rightarrow
Genomic\ Variation
$$

By analyzing:

- heterozygosity
- nucleotide diversity
- Site Frequency Spectra
- linkage disequilibrium
- IBD tracts
- whole-genome genealogies

we can estimate:

- historical effective population size ($N_e$)
- bottlenecks
- population expansions
- divergence times
- migration rates
- admixture events

Modern demographic inference therefore treats the genome as a historical archive in which patterns of variation preserve information about population history over thousands to millions of generations.

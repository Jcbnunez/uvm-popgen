# 1. The Central Question

Evolutionary change is driven by several forces, but two of the most important are:

- Natural selection
- Genetic drift

Selection causes predictable changes in allele frequencies because some genotypes leave more offspring than others.

Drift causes random changes because populations are finite and reproduction is a sampling process.

The fundamental question is:

> When is selection strong enough to overcome random drift?

The answer depends largely on two quantities:

$$
N_e
$$

and

$$
s
$$

where:

- $N_e$ = effective population size
- $s$ = selection coefficient

---

# 2. Review of Genetic Drift

Consider an allele $\alpha_i$ with frequency

$$
p_t
$$

in generation $t$.

Under random sampling, the number of copies in the next generation follows a binomial process.

For a diploid population there are

$$
2N_e
$$

gene copies.

The variance in allele frequency after one generation is

$$
Var(p_{t+1})=
\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N_e}
$$

and therefore the standard deviation is

$$
SD(p_{t+1})=
\sqrt{\frac{p_t(1-p_t)}{2N_e}}
$$

### Biological Interpretation

Drift becomes stronger as:

$$
N_e
$$

becomes smaller.

Thus small populations experience larger random fluctuations than large populations.

---

# 3. Diploid Viability Selection Model

Consider a locus $\alpha$ with two alleles:

- $\alpha_i$
- $\alpha_j$

Let

$$
p=f(\alpha_i)
$$

and

$$
q=f(\alpha_j)
$$

where

$$
p+q=1
$$

Assume genotype fitnesses:

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|-----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | $1+s$ |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | $1+hs$ |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | $1$ |

where:

- $s$ = selection coefficient
- $h$ = dominance coefficient

---

## Dominance Cases

| $h$ | Biological Meaning |
|-------|-------|
| 0 | Completely recessive |
| 0.5 | Additive |
| 1 | Completely dominant |

---

# 4. Selection Versus Drift

Recall that, selection produces allele-frequency change:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{spq[p+h(q-p)]}
{\bar w}
$$

Drift produces fluctuations with magnitude approximately

$$
SD(p_{t+1})=
\sqrt{\frac{pq}{2N_e}}
$$

The evolutionary outcome depends upon which force is larger.

---

# 5. Behavior of Rare Alleles

Most new mutations begin as a single copy.

Their initial frequency is

$$
p=\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

and therefore

$$
q \approx 1
$$

Substituting into the exact expression:

$$
\Delta p
\approx
hp\s
$$

Thus the heterozygote determines the initial effectiveness of selection.

This is arguably the most important result of diploid selection theory.

---

# 6. The Drift-Selection Threshold

For a rare allele,

$$
\Delta p
\approx
hp\s
$$

while drift generates fluctuations approximately proportional to

$$
\frac{1}{N_e}
$$

Selection becomes effective when deterministic change exceeds random noise.

This occurs when

$$
N_e|hs| \approx 1
$$

More generally:

Selection dominates:

$$
N_e|hs| \gg 1
$$

Drift dominates:

$$
N_e|hs| \ll 1
$$

---

# 7. Derivation of the Selection-Drift Equilibrium ($h = 1$)

The classic population genetics identity defines the boundary where natural selection overcomes genetic drift[cite: 1]. Setting $h = 1$ (meaning $hs = s$), here is the step-by-step algebraic derivation.

---

### Step 1: Deterministic Change Due to Selection ($\Delta p_{\text{sel}}$)

#### Assume
Consider a diploid locus with two alleles, $A$ (frequency $p$) and $a$ (frequency $q = 1-p$). Under standard viability selection, the relative fitnesses of the genotypes are:
* **$AA$**: $1 + s$
* **$Aa$**: $1 + s$ (Setting $h = 1$)
* **$aa$**: $1$

Recall

Expand and simplify the numerator:
$$\Delta p_{\text{sel}} = \frac{sp(1-p)^2}{1 + sp(2-p)}$$

#### Weak Selection Approximation
Under weak selection ($s \ll 1$), the denominator $1 + sp(2-p) \approx 1$. 

Thus,

$$\Delta p_{\text{sel}} \approx s \cdot p(1-p)$$


### Step 2: Stochastic Variance Due to Genetic Drift ($\sigma_{\Delta p}$)

In a finite population of effective size $N_e$, random sampling of gametes causes fluctuations in allele frequency. The standard deviation (magnitude of expected random noise) per generation is:

$$\sigma_{\Delta p} = \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e}}$$

### Step 3: Equating Selection and Drift



$$
sp(1-p)\gtrsim \sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e}}
$$



The goal is to ask:

> How strong must selection be before it becomes larger than the random fluctuations generated by drift?

---

### Step 4. Remove the Square Root

Square both sides:

$$
[s\,p(1-p)]^2
\gtrsim
\frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e}
$$

Expanding the left side:

$$
s^2p^2(1-p)^2
\gtrsim
\frac{p(1-p)}{2N_e}
$$

---

### Step 5. Divide by $p(1-p)$

to obtain

$$
s^2p(1-p)
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

---

### Step 6. Solve for $Nₑs²$

Multiply both sides by

$$
2N_e
$$

giving

$$
2N_es^2p(1-p)
\gtrsim
1
$$

We can reorganize this to show, that for selection to overcome drift, **from one generation to the next (i.e., step-by-step effects),** it must be (recall that $(1-p)=q$):

$$
s^2pq
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

---

# Revised Interpretation of the Drift-Selection Threshold

Population genetics texts often summarize the drift-selection boundary as

$$
|hs| \gtrsim \frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

or equivalently

$$
2N_e|hs| \gtrsim 1
$$

for diploid populations.

This result comes from a fixation-probability argument rather than from comparing one generation of deterministic change to one generation of drift.

The intuition is straightforward.

A new mutation begins at frequency

$$
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

and therefore has a neutral fixation probability of

$$
P_{fix}^{neutral}=
\frac{1}{2N_e}.
$$

Selection only becomes evolutionarily important when it changes the fixation probability by an amount comparable to this neutral baseline.

For a newly arisen mutation, the selective effect is experienced through heterozygotes, and therefore scales with

$$
hs.
$$

As a result, selection becomes effective when

$$
|hs|
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

or, equivalently,

$$
2N_e|hs|
\gtrsim
1.
$$

This threshold marks the approximate boundary between effectively neutral evolution and selection-dominated evolution.

---

# 8. Why Dominance Matters

A new mutation is almost always found in heterozygotes because it initially exists as only a single copy in the population.

Therefore selection first acts on the heterozygote fitness

$$
1+hs
$$

rather than on the homozygote fitness

$$
1+s.
$$

Consequently, the evolutionary visibility of a new mutation depends on its dominance coefficient.

The quantity governing the efficacy of selection is therefore

$$
2N_e|hs|
$$

rather than simply

$$
2N_e|s|.
$$

This creates major differences among dominant, additive, and recessive mutations.

---

# 9. Beneficial Dominant Mutations

Suppose

$$
s>0
$$

and

$$
h=1.
$$

Fitnesses are

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|-----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | $1+s$ |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | $1+s$ |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | $1$ |

A newly arisen mutation immediately experiences its full selective advantage.

Selection becomes effective when

$$
s
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

or equivalently

$$
2N_es
\gtrsim
1.
$$

Because the mutation is visible to selection from the moment it appears, dominant beneficial alleles are relatively likely to escape loss by drift.

---

# 10. Beneficial Recessive Mutations

Suppose

$$
s>0
$$

and

$$
h=0.
$$

Fitnesses become

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|-----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | $1+s$ |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | $1$ |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | $1$ |

When rare, the mutation occurs almost exclusively as a heterozygote.

Because heterozygotes have no fitness advantage,

$$
hs=0.
$$

Selection therefore cannot initially distinguish the mutation from a neutral allele.

The mutation must first drift to a higher frequency before homozygotes appear and selection can act strongly.

This phenomenon is known as the **Haldane sieve**.

Thus many beneficial recessive mutations are lost by drift before selection ever has an opportunity to favor them.

---

# 11. Deleterious Mutations

Suppose

$$
s<0.
$$

For dominant deleterious alleles:

$$
h \approx 1
$$

and heterozygotes immediately experience a fitness reduction.

Selection therefore removes the allele efficiently whenever

$$
|s|
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}.
$$

For recessive deleterious alleles:

$$
h \approx 0
$$

and heterozygotes conceal the mutation from selection.

When rare, the allele behaves nearly neutrally and can persist in the population for many generations.

Only after homozygotes become sufficiently common does selection act strongly against it.

This explains why natural populations often harbor many rare recessive harmful alleles.

---

# 12. Selection-Drift Balance and Nearly Neutral Theory

Tomoko Ohta's Nearly Neutral Theory focuses on mutations whose fitness effects are so small that their evolutionary fate depends on population size.

The boundary occurs approximately when

$$
|hs|
\approx
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

or

$$
2N_e|hs|
\approx
1.
$$

Such mutations occupy the nearly neutral zone.

In small populations they behave almost neutrally because drift dominates.

In large populations the same mutations experience effective selection.

Thus the same mutation may be effectively neutral in one species but strongly selected in another.

---

# 13. Consequences for Different Population Sizes

## Large Populations

Large values of

$$
N_e
$$

produce

$$
2N_e|hs|
\gg
1.
$$

Consequences include:

- efficient adaptation
- efficient purifying selection
- rapid removal of mildly deleterious alleles
- reduced mutation load

Even mutations with very small fitness effects may be visible to selection.

---

## Small Populations

Small values of

$$
N_e
$$

produce

$$
2N_e|hs|
\ll
1.
$$

Consequences include:

- stronger genetic drift
- less effective adaptation
- inefficient purifying selection
- increased accumulation of mildly deleterious variants
- greater mutation load

Many mutations that would be selected in large populations become effectively neutral.

---

# 14. Practical Rule

When evaluating a new mutation in a diploid population, calculate

$$
2N_e|hs|.
$$

### If

$$
2N_e|hs| < 1
$$

the mutation is effectively neutral and drift dominates.

### If

$$
2N_e|hs| \approx 1
$$

the mutation lies in the nearly neutral zone.

### If

$$
2N_e|hs| > 10
$$

selection is generally effective and evolutionary outcomes become increasingly deterministic.

---

# Summary Table

| Condition | Interpretation |
|------------|-------------|
| $2N_ehs \ll 1$ | Drift dominates |
| $2N_ehs \approx 1$ | Nearly neutral boundary |
| $2N_ehs \gg 1$ | Selection dominates |
| $h=1$ | Dominant mutation |
| $h=0.5$ | Additive mutation |
| $h=0$ | Recessive mutation |

# Grand Synthesis

For diploid populations, viability selection produces the exact allele-frequency recursion

$$
p'=
\frac{p^2(1+s)+pq(1+hs)}
{p^2(1+s)+2pq(1+hs)+q^2}
$$

and the corresponding allele-frequency change

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{spq[p+h(q-p)]}
{\bar w}.
$$

Genetic drift introduces random fluctuations with variance

$$
Var(p_{t+1})=
\frac{pq}{2N_e}
$$

and standard deviation

$$
SD(p_{t+1})=
\sqrt{\frac{pq}{2N_e}}.
$$

The ultimate evolutionary success of a mutation, however, is determined by its fixation probability.

A newly arisen mutation begins at frequency

$$
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

and therefore has a neutral fixation probability of

$$
P_{fix} = \frac{1}{2N_e}.
$$

Selection becomes evolutionarily important when it alters that fixation probability by an amount comparable to the neutral expectation.

This occurs when

$$
|hs|
\gtrsim
\frac{1}{2N_e}
$$

or equivalently

$$
2N_e|hs|
\gtrsim
1.
$$

This result explains:

- why dominance affects evolutionary trajectories,
- why dominant beneficial mutations are more readily established,
- why recessive beneficial mutations suffer from Haldane's sieve,
- why recessive deleterious mutations can persist at low frequencies,
- why effective population size determines the efficacy of selection,
- and why the quantity

$$
2N_e|hs|
$$

serves as the classical measure of selection relative to drift in diploid populations.

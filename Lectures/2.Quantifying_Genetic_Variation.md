# Important Formulas

## From theory to expectation (prediction)

Expected genotype frequencies under Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium:

$$
f(x_{ii}) = p \times p = p^2
$$

$$
f(x_{ij}) = qp + pq = 2pq
$$

$$
f(x_{jj}) = q \times q = q^2
$$

---

## From observed samples to inferred frequencies

Estimating allele frequencies from genotype frequencies:

$$
p = x_{ii} + \frac{1}{2}x_{ij}
$$

$$
q = x_{jj} + \frac{1}{2}x_{ij}
$$

### Predicting future data

Use expected genotype frequencies when predicting what future data may look like.

### Working with past data

Use inferred allele frequencies when analyzing observed data.

---

# Estimating Allele Frequencies

## From frequencies

$$
f_i = f(x_{ii}) + \frac{1}{2}f(x_{ij})
$$

## From counts

For a sample of size \(n\):

$$
f_i = \frac{2x_{ii} + x_{ij}}{2n}
$$

---

# A Problem: Inferred Estimates Have Error

Recall that bi-allelic loci can be modeled as a binomial process.

The confidence interval for a binomial proportion is:

$$
CI = p \pm \gamma\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

where:

- $p$ = allele frequency estimate
- $n$ = sample size
- $\gamma$ = critical value from the normal distribution
- For a 95% confidence interval, ($\gamma$ = 1.96)

### What is n?

Depending on the analysis:

- Number of individuals sampled

- Number of chromosomes sampled $2N$

Since diploid individuals contribute two chromosomes, population genetic analyses often use chromosome counts.

# Example: Monitoring a Pollution Marker

Suppose we measure allele frequencies at a pollution marker gene ("pol") through time.

Observed frequencies:

- Year (t): $p_t = 0.10$

- Year (t+1): $p_{t+1} = 0.21$

Observed change:

$$
\Delta p = 0.11
$$

Question:

Is this change statistically significant at the 95% confidence level?

---

# How Many Fish Do We Need?

We want to know whether:

$$
p_t = 0.10
$$

and

$$
p_{t+1} = 0.21
$$

are statistically distinguishable.
If the observed difference can be explained by sampling error, it is not biologically interesting.
If the difference exceeds expected sampling error, we can investigate evolutionary explanations.

---

# Confidence Intervals and Statistical Power

Binomial confidence interval:

$$
CI = p \pm 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

Upper limit:

$$
p + 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

Lower limit:

$$
p - 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}
$$

Interpretation:

- If the estimate from year \(t+1\) falls within the confidence interval of year \(t\), the difference is not significant.
- If it falls outside, the difference is significant.

---

# Solving for Sample Size

We want a confidence interval width equal to the observed difference:

$$
0.11
$$

Start with:

$$
(p + 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}})
(p - 1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}})=0.11
$$

Simplify:

$$
1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}+1.96\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}=0.11
$$

$$
3.92\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}=0.11
$$

$$
\sqrt{\frac{p(1-p)}{n}}=0.02806
$$

Square both sides:

$$
\frac{p(1-p)}{n}=0.000787
$$

Expand:

$$
\frac{p-p^2}{n}=0.000787
$$

Solve for \(n\):

$$
n=\frac{p-p^2}{0.000787}
$$

---

# Example Calculation

Using the original allele frequency:

$$
p = 0.10
$$

Substitute:

$$
n =
\frac{0.10-(0.10)^2}{0.000787}
$$

$$
n =
\frac{0.10-0.01}{0.000787}
$$

$$
n =\frac{0.09}{0.000787}
$$

$$
n = 114
$$

Result:

$$
n = 114
$$

Therefore, we require:
- 114 individuals
- 228 chromosomes

to detect a change from:

$$
p = 0.10
$$

to

$$
p = 0.21
$$

at the 95% confidence level.

# What If We Sample Only 100 Fish?

With only 100 individuals:
- Statistical power is insufficient.
- A biologically meaningful change may not be detected.
- Failure to reject the null hypothesis may reflect inadequate sample size rather than absence of change.
Key conclusion:
WE DO NOT HAVE ENOUGH POWER TO DETECT A MEANINGFUL CHANGE.

# Why Do Allele Frequencies Change?

Allele frequencies can change for two broad reasons.

## 1. Sampling Noise

Changes arise because:
- Samples are finite.
- Statistical estimates contain error.
- Studies may lack sufficient power.
These changes do not necessarily reflect evolution.

## 2. Evolution

Changes arise because allele frequencies truly shift through time.
Evolutionary causes include:

### Natural Selection

Alleles differ in fitness, causing predictable frequency changes.

### Neutral Processes

Frequency changes occur through processes such as:

- Genetic drift
- Demographic fluctuations
- Founder effects
- Population bottlenecks

# Key Takeaways

1. Genotype frequencies can be predicted from allele frequencies:

$$
p^2, 
2pq,
q^2
$$

2. Allele frequencies can be inferred from genotype observations.

3. Sampling error creates uncertainty around frequency estimates.

4. Confidence intervals quantify this uncertainty.

5. Statistical power depends strongly on sample size.

6. Detecting evolutionary change requires distinguishing true biological change from sampling noise.

7. Allele frequency changes can reflect:

- Sampling error
- Natural selection
- Neutral evolutionary processes

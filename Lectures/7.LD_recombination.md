# Introduction

Population geneticists are interested not only in allele frequencies at individual loci, but also in the association of alleles across loci.

Consider two biallelic loci:

| Locus A | Locus B |
|----------|----------|
| $A_i$ / $A_j$ | $B_i$ / $B_j$ |

There are four possible haplotypes:

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $A_i$ $B_i$ | $x_1$ |
| $A_i$ $B_j$ | $x_2$ |
| $A_j$ $B_i$ | $x_3$ |
| $A_j$ $B_j$ | $x_4$ |

such that

$$
x_1 + x_2 + x_3 + x_4 = 1
$$

The allele frequencies are

$$
p_{A_i} = x_1 + x_2
$$

$$
p_{A_j} = x_3 + x_4
$$

$$
p_{B_i} = x_1 + x_3
$$

$$
p_{B_j} = x_2 + x_4
$$

---

# Linkage Equilibrium

Two loci are in linkage equilibrium when alleles combine independently.

Under linkage equilibrium,

$$
P(A_iB_i) = P(A_i)P(B_i)
$$

or

$$
x_1 = p_{A_i}p_{B_i}
$$

Similarly,

$$
x_2 = p_{A_i}p_{B_j}
$$

$$
x_3 = p_{A_j}p_{B_i}
$$

$$
x_4 = p_{A_j}p_{B_j}
$$

## Example

Suppose

$$
p_{A_i}=0.5
$$

and

$$
p_{B_i}=0.5
$$

Then

| Haplotype | Expected Frequency |
|------------|-------------------|
| $A_i$ $B_i$ | $0.25$ |
| $A_i$ $B_j$ | $0.25$ |
| $A_j$ $B_i$ | $0.25$ |
| $A_j$ $B_j$ | $0.25$ |

Knowledge of the allele at one locus provides no information about the allele at the other locus.

---

# Linkage Disequilibrium

Now suppose the haplotype frequencies are

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $A_i$ $B_i$ | $0.40$ |
| $A_i$ $B_j$ | $0.10$ |
| $A_j$ $B_i$ | $0.10$ |
| $A_j$ $B_j$ | $0.40$ |

The allele frequencies remain

$$
p_{A_i}=0.5
$$

$$
p_{B_i}=0.5
$$

If loci were independent, we would expect

$$
P(A_iB_i)=0.25
$$

Instead,

$$
P(A_iB_i)=0.40
$$

The $A_i$ $B_i$ haplotype occurs more often than expected.

The loci are therefore in linkage disequilibrium.

---

# Measuring Linkage Disequilibrium

## The LD Coefficient ($D$)

The simplest measure of LD is

$$
D = P(A_iB_i) - P(A_i)P(B_i)
$$

or

$$
D = x_1 - p_{A_i}p_{B_i}
$$

### Example

Observed frequency:

$$
x_1 = 0.40
$$

Expected frequency:

$$
p_{A_i}p_{B_i}=0.25
$$

Therefore

$$
D = 0.40 - 0.25
$$

$$
D = 0.15
$$

A positive value indicates that $A_i$ tends to occur with $B_i$ and $A_j$ tends to occur with $B_j$.

---

## Alternative Expression for $D$

An equivalent formula is

$$
D = x_1x_4 - x_2x_3
$$

For our example,

$$
D=(0.40)(0.40)-(0.10)(0.10)
$$

$$
D=0.16-0.01
$$

$$
D=0.15
$$

---

# Why Raw $D$ Is Not Ideal

The maximum possible value of $D$ depends on allele frequencies.

For example, if

$$
p_{A_i}=p_{B_i}=0.95
$$

the maximum achievable value of $D$ is much smaller than when

$$
p_{A_i}=p_{B_i}=0.50
$$

As a result, values of $D$ cannot be compared easily among loci.

---

# Standardized LD: $D'$

To remove allele-frequency dependence,

$$
D' = \frac{D}{D_{max}}
$$

Properties:

$$
-1 \le D' \le 1
$$

Interpretation:

- $D' = 1$ indicates complete positive association.
- $D' = -1$ indicates complete negative association.
- $D' = 0$ indicates no association.

---

# Correlation-Based LD: $r^2$

The most commonly used LD statistic is

$$
r^2 =\frac{D^2}{p_{A_i}p_{A_j}p_{B_i}p_{B_j}}
$$

Properties:

$$
0 \le r^2 \le 1
$$

Interpretation:

- $r^2 = 1$ means perfect predictability.
- $r^2 = 0$ means no predictability.

---

## Example

Suppose

$$
D = 0.15
$$

and

$$
p_{A_i}=p_{B_i}=0.5
$$

Then

$$
r^2=\frac{0.15^2}
{(0.5)(0.5)(0.5)(0.5)}
$$

$$
r^2=\frac{0.0225}{0.0625}
$$

$$
r^2=0.36
$$

Thus 36% of the variation at one SNP predicts variation at the other SNP.

---

# Physical Linkage Versus Linkage Disequilibrium

These concepts are related but distinct.

## Physical Linkage

Physical linkage is a property of chromosomes.

Two loci are physically linked if they occur near one another on the chromosome.

Physical linkage is measured in:

- base pairs

- centimorgans (cM)

- recombination fractions

## Linkage Disequilibrium

LD is a property of populations.

It measures statistical association between alleles.

Two loci can be:

- physically linked but show weak LD

- physically distant but show substantial LD

depending on population history and selection.

---

# Recombination

Consider two haplotypes:

| Chromosome 1 | Chromosome 2 |
|--------------|--------------|
| $A_i$ $B_i$ | $A_j$ $B_j$ |

A crossover occurring between the loci produces:

| Recombinant Haplotypes |
|------------------------|
| $A_i$ $B_j$ |
| $A_j$ $B_i$ |

Recombination generates new haplotypes.

---

# The Recombination Fraction

The recombination fraction is

$$
\rho
$$

where

$$
0 \le \rho \le 0.5
$$

Interpretation:

| $\rho$ | Meaning |
|---|----------|
| 0 | Complete linkage |
| 0.5 | Independent assortment |

Nearby loci tend to have small values of $\rho$.

Widely separated loci approach

$$
\rho = 0.5
$$

---

# Recombination Destroys Linkage Disequilibrium

Suppose LD at generation $t$ is

$$
D_t
$$

A fraction $r$ of chromosome copies recombine each generation.

The recursion equation is

$$
D_{t+1}=(1-\rho)D_t
$$

This is one of the most important equations in population genetics.

Each generation, recombination removes a fraction $\rho$ of the existing LD.

---

# Exponential Decay of LD

Applying the recursion repeatedly,

$$
D_1=(1-\rho)D_0
$$

$$
D_2=(1-\rho)^2D_0
$$

After $t$ generations,

$$
D_t=D_0(1-\rho)^t
$$

LD decays exponentially through time.

---

## Time to Complete Decay of LD

Starting with the recursion

$$
D_t=D_0(1-\rho)^t
$$

we can ask:

> How long does it take for LD to decay to some fraction $f$ of its original value?

Set

$$
D_t=fD_0
$$

Substituting into the decay equation:

$$
fD_0=D_0(1-\rho)^t
$$

Cancel $D_0$ from both sides:

$$
f=(1-\rho)^t
$$

Taking natural logarithms:

$$
\ln(f)=t\ln(1-\rho)
$$

Solving for $t$:

$$
t=\frac{\ln(f)}
{\ln(1-\rho)}
$$

This is the general solution.

---

### Half-Life of LD

For 50% decay,

$$
f=0.5
$$

therefore

$$
t_{1/2}=\frac{\ln(0.5)}
{\ln(1-\rho)}
$$

For small $\rho$,

$$
\ln(1-\rho)\approx-\rho
$$

so

$$
t_{1/2}\approx\frac{0.693}{\rho}
$$

---

### 90% Decay of LD

If we define 90% decay as only 10% of the original LD remaining,

$$
f=0.1
$$

then

$$
t_{90}=
\frac{\ln(0.1)}
{\ln(1-\rho)}
$$

For small $\rho$,

$$
t_{90}
\approx
\frac{2.303}{\rho}
$$

---


### Complete Decay

Strictly speaking,

$$
D_t=D_0(1-\rho)^t
$$

approaches zero asymptotically and never actually reaches

$$
D_t=0
$$

for any finite value of $t$ (unless $\rho=1$).

Thus:

$$
\lim_{t\to\infty} D_t = 0
$$

and the "time to complete decay" is

$$
t=\infty.
$$

In practice, population geneticists instead define decay using thresholds such as:

- 50% remaining (half-life)
- 10% remaining ($t_{90}$)
- 1% remaining ($t_{99}$)

because true complete decay never occurs in finite time.

# Evolutionary Forces That Generate LD

Because recombination destroys LD, other forces must continually create it.

## Mutation

A new mutation arises on a specific haplotype background.

Initially, it is perfectly associated with nearby alleles.

Thus every new mutation begins in strong local LD.

## Genetic Drift

Random sampling creates nonrandom associations among loci.

Drift is especially important in:

- small populations

- bottlenecked populations

- founder populations

Drift continuously generates LD.

## Population Admixture

Suppose two populations differ in allele frequencies.

Population 1:

$$
p_{A_i}=p_{B_i}=0.9
$$

Population 2:

$$
p_{A_i}=p_{B_i}=0.1
$$

After mixing, positive LD appears automatically even if neither population initially exhibited LD.

## Natural Selection

Selection often increases the frequency of specific haplotypes.

As a result, selection commonly generates LD.

---

# Selective Sweeps and LD

Suppose a beneficial mutation arises on one chromosome.

Selection rapidly increases the frequency of that chromosome.

Nearby neutral variants hitchhike with the advantageous mutation.

Consequences include:

- elevated LD

- long haplotypes

- reduced nucleotide diversity

These patterns are classic signatures of recent positive selection.


# Summary of Key Equations

$$
D=P(A_iB_i)-P(A_i)P(B_i)
$$

$$
D=x_1x_4-x_2x_3
$$

$$
D'=\frac{D}{D_{max}}
$$

$$
r^2=\frac{D^2}{p_{A_i}p_{A_j}p_{B_i}p_{B_j}}
$$

$$
D_{t+1}=(1-r)D_t
$$

$$
D_t=D_0(1-r)^t
$$

# Big Picture

Linkage disequilibrium is the statistical association among alleles at different loci.

Mutation, drift, admixture, and selection generate LD.

Recombination destroys LD.

The genomic patterns we observe reflect the balance between these competing forces.

Understanding LD is fundamental for:

- selective sweep detection
- demographic inference
- effective population size estimation
- recombination mapping
- haplotype-based analyses of adaptation

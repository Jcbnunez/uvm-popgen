# What Is a Model of Evolutionary Change?

A model is a tool that combines biological assumptions with mathematical formulations.

Models are used to:

1. Predict future evolutionary change

2. Explain past observations

Models are simplifications of reality. Their purpose is not to perfectly recreate nature, but to capture its essential features.

---

# Elements of a Population Genetics Model

Every model contains:

## Architecture

The genetic assumptions of the model.

## Ecology

The world in which genetic variation exists and interacts.

## Goal

A first principle or prediction generated by the model.

---

# The Hardy-Weinberg Model

The Hardy-Weinberg (H-W) model is one of the foundational models in population genetics.

## Architecture Assumptions

- Individuals are diploid

- One gene is being considered

- The gene has \(n\) alleles

- Allele frequencies sum to 1

- Both alleles exist initially

- No new mutations occur

- Population size is effectively infinite

## Ecological Assumptions

- No natural selection

- No competition

- No predation

- No mate choice

- No random mortality

- No migration

---

# Summary of Hardy-Weinberg Assumptions

1. Random mating
2. Infinite population size
3. No mutation
4. No migration
5. No natural selection

Under these assumptions, allele and genotype frequencies remain constant through time.

---

# Why Is Hardy-Weinberg Useful?

Hardy-Weinberg describes a world in which evolution does not occur.

This is useful because it provides a null expectation against which real populations can be compared.

If populations deviate from Hardy-Weinberg expectations, evolutionary forces may be acting.

Hardy-Weinberg also serves as the mathematical foundation for more complex evolutionary models.

---

# A Formal Definition of Hardy-Weinberg

The generalized Hardy-Weinberg model predicts that under conditions where no evolutionary processes act, genotype frequencies after one generation of random mating can be predicted using multinomial expansions.

The frequencies of all alleles sum to one:

$$
\sum_{i=1}^{n} p_i = 1
$$

where:

- $p_i$ represents the frequency of allele $i$

- $n$ is the number of alleles

---

# Two-Allele Case

For two alleles:

$$
p + q = 1
$$

The Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium follows from the binomial expansion:

$$
(p+q)^2=p^2 + 2pq + q^2
$$

---

# Binomial Expansions and Ploidy

### Haploid

$$
(p+q)^1 = p + q
$$

### Diploid

$$
(p+q)^2 = p^2 + 2pq + q^2
$$

### Triploid

$$
(p+q)^3=p^3 + 3p^2q + 3pq^2 + q^3
$$

### Tetraploid

$$
(p+q)^4=p^4 + 4p^3q + 6p^2q^2 + 4pq^3 + q^4
$$

Each expansion sums to 1.

---

# Interpretation of the Diploid Expansion

Assume two alleles:

- allele $i$
- allele $j$

Possible genotypes:

### Homozygotes

$$
x_{ii}
$$

$$
x_{jj}
$$

### Heterozygotes

$$
x_{ij}
$$

The expected genotype frequencies are:

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ii}) = p^2
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ij}) = 2pq
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{jj}) = q^2
$$

where:

$$
p = \text{freq}(i)
$$

$$
q = \text{freq}(j)
$$

---

# Triploid Interpretation

For triploid organisms:

$$
(p+q)^3=p^3 + 3p^2q + 3pq^2 + q^3
$$

Expected genotype frequencies:

$$
\text{freq}(x_{iii}) = p^3
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{iij}) = 3p^2q
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ijj}) = 3pq^2
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{jjj}) = q^3
$$

---

# The Three Allele Case

Suppose a diploid gene contains three alleles:

- $i$
- $j$
- $k$

Allele frequencies:

$$
p = \text{freq}(i)
$$

$$
q = \text{freq}(j)
$$

$$
r = \text{freq}(k)
$$

with

$$
p+q+r = 1
$$

---

# Trinomial Expansion

The expected genotype frequencies follow:

$$
(p+q+r)^2=p^2 + q^2 + r^2 + 2pq + 2pr + 2qr
$$

---

# Genotype Frequencies for Three Alleles

### Homozygotes

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ii}) = p^2
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{jj}) = q^2
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{kk}) = r^2
$$

### Heterozygotes

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ij}) = 2pq
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{ik}) = 2pr
$$

$$
\text{freq}(x_{jk}) = 2qr
$$

---

# Connecting Genetics and Evolution

Hardy-Weinberg provides expectations at both:

## Allelic Level

$$
E[f_i] = p
$$

$$
E[f_j] = q
$$

## Genotypic Level

$$
E[x_{ii}] = p^2
$$

$$
E[x_{ij}] = 2pq
$$

$$
E[x_{jj}] = q^2
$$

Observed genotype frequencies can be used to infer allele frequencies.

For a diploid population:

$$
p=x_{ii}+\frac{1}{2}x_{ij}
$$

---

# Evolution Is a Time-Dependent Process

Time is the independent variable.

Evolutionary biology studies:

- Levels of genetic variation
- Causes of genetic variation
- Consequences of genetic variation

all through time.

---

# Defining Evolutionary Change

We define evolutionary change generally as:

$$
\text{Evol}=\frac{\Delta x}{\Delta t}
$$

where:
- $x$ is some evolutionary metric
- $t$ is time

---

# Measuring Evolution One Generation at a Time

For simplicity:

$$
\Delta t = 1
$$

and therefore

$$
\text{Evol}=\Delta(\text{evolutionary metric})
$$

where

$$
\Delta x=x_{final}-x_{initial}
$$

---

# A Common Metric: Allele Frequency Change

One of the most useful measures of evolution is change in allele frequency.

Let:

$$
p_t=\text{allele frequency at generation } t
$$

Then:

$$
\Delta p=p_{t+1}-p_t
$$

Thus:

$$
\text{Evol}=\Delta p
$$

or

$$
\text{Evol}=\frac{\Delta p}{\Delta t}
$$

Since \(\Delta t=1\),

$$
\text{Evol}=\Delta p
$$

---

# Coral Example

Assume:

- Diploid population
- Two alleles
- Random mating
- Very large population
- No mutation
- No migration
- No selection

Suppose:

$$
p = 0.6
$$

$$
q = 0.4
$$

Predicted offspring genotype frequencies:

$$
x_{ii} = p^2 = 0.36
$$

$$
x_{ij} = 2pq = 0.48
$$

$$
x_{jj} = q^2 = 0.16
$$

These sum to:

$$
0.36 + 0.48 + 0.16 = 1
$$

---

# Recovering Allele Frequencies

Calculate the frequency of allele $i$:

$$
p=x_{ii}+\frac{1}{2}x_{ij}
$$

Substituting:

$$
p=0.36 + \frac{0.48}{2}
$$

$$
p=0.36 + 0.24
$$

$$
p=0.60
$$

Therefore:

$$
p_{t+1}=0.60
$$

and

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

No evolution has occurred.

---

# Key Takeaways

1. Hardy-Weinberg is a null model of evolution.
2. Under Hardy-Weinberg assumptions, allele frequencies do not change.
3. Genotype frequencies can be predicted from allele frequencies.
4. Evolution can be measured as changes in allele frequency through time.
5. Deviations from Hardy-Weinberg expectations suggest evolutionary forces are acting.

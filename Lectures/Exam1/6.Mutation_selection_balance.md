# 1. Mutation as an Evolutionary Force

Up to this point, we have focused on natural selection.

Today we introduce a new evolutionary force:

> Mutation

Mutation introduces new genetic variation into populations through errors in DNA replication and repair. 

Unlike selection, mutation does not sort variation.

Instead, mutation creates variation that selection can later act upon.

---

# 2. Why Mutation is Considered a Weak Force

A mutation occurs when one nucleotide changes into another.

For example:

```text
ACTGA
ACTTA
```

Mutation events are largely random with respect to fitness, but occur at approximately constant rates. Because mutation rates are typically very low, mutation alone changes allele frequencies slowly. Typical mutation rates are on the order of:

$$
10^{-8}\text{ to }10^{-6}
$$

per nucleotide per generation.

For this reason, mutation is often described as a weak evolutionary force. Nevertheless, because mutation is statistical and predictable, it can be modeled mathematically. 

---

# 3. Modeling Mutation

Let

$$
\mu
$$

be the probability that allele $i$ mutates into allele $j$.

Then

$$
\text{Prob}(i \rightarrow j)=\mu
$$

Mutation removes copies of allele \(i\).

Therefore,

$$
p_{t+1}=p(1-\mu)
$$

This equation states that the frequency of allele \(i\) in the next generation depends on the probability that it does **not** mutate.

---

# 4. Back Mutation

A second possibility is that allele \(j\) mutates back into allele \(i\).

Let

$$
\nu
$$

represent the back mutation rate.

Then

$$
\text{Prob}(j \rightarrow i)=\nu
$$

Back mutation adds copies of allele \(i\).

Combining both processes gives

$$
p_{t+1}=p(1-\mu)+q\nu
$$

where:

- $p$ is the frequency of allele $i$
- $q$ is the frequency of allele $j$

---

# 5. Mutation Equilibrium

An equilibrium occurs whenever allele frequencies stop changing:

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

Recall:

$$
\Delta p=p_{t+1}-p
$$

Substituting the mutation equation:

$$
0=p(1-\mu)+q\nu-p
$$

Using

$$
q=1-p
$$

gives

$$
0=p(1-\mu)+(1-p)\nu-p
$$

Expanding:

$$
0=p-p\mu+\nu-p\nu-p
$$

Cancel $p$ terms:

$$
0=-p\mu+\nu-p\nu
$$

Rearrange:

$$
p\mu+p\nu=\nu
$$

Factor:

$$
p(\mu+\nu)=\nu
$$

Solve for $p$:

$$
\hat p=\frac{\nu}{\mu+\nu}
$$

This is the equilibrium expected under mutation alone.

---

# 6. Most New Mutations Are Deleterious

Empirical studies generally show that:

- Most mutations are effectively neutral.
- Of the mutations that affect fitness, most are deleterious.
- Beneficial mutations are relatively rare.

This observation raises an important question:

> Why are most functional mutations deleterious?

Two common explanations are:
1. Organisms are often adapted to their environments, so random changes are more likely to reduce fitness than improve it.
2. Random mutations can disrupt the normal function of genes and regulatory elements.

---

# 7. Purifying Selection

Purifying selection occurs when deleterious mutations are removed from a population.

Under purifying selection:

- Mutation continuously introduces harmful alleles.
- Selection continuously removes harmful alleles.

Because these forces act in opposite directions, neither usually wins completely.

Instead, populations often approach an equilibrium known as:

> Mutation-selection balance

---

# 8. Modeling Mutation-Selection Balance

Assume mutation and selection act independently.

Then the total change in allele frequency is:

$$
\Delta p=\Delta_s p+\Delta_\mu p
$$

where:
- $\Delta_s p$ is change due to selection
- $\Delta_\mu p$ is change due to mutation


---

# 9. Change Due to Selection

From our dominance model,

$$
\Delta_s p=
\frac{pqs_{jj}\left[ph+q(1-h)\right]}
{1-2pqhs_{jj}-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

Now make two simplifying assumptions:

1. Complete dominance:

$$
h=0
$$

2. The deleterious allele is rare:

$$
q \approx 0
$$

Substituting \(h=0\):

$$
\Delta_s p=\frac{pq^2s_{jj}}
{1-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

Because $q$ is very small:

$$
q^2 \approx 0
$$

Thus,

$$
1-q^2s_{jj}\approx 1
$$

and

$$
\Delta_s p
\approx
pq^2s_{jj}
$$

---

# 10. Change Due to Mutation

Recall the mutation equation:

$$
\Delta p=p(1-\mu)+q\nu-p
$$

Assume back mutation is negligible:

$$
\nu \approx 0
$$

Then:

$$
\Delta_\mu p=p(1-\mu)-p
$$

Expanding:

$$
\Delta_\mu p=p-p\mu-p
$$

Therefore:

$$
\Delta_\mu p=-p\mu
$$

---

# 11. Combining Selection and Mutation

Substituting the approximations for selection and mutation:

$$
\Delta p=pq^2s_{jj}-p\mu
$$

At equilibrium:

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

Therefore

$$
0=pq^2s_{jj}-p\mu
$$

Factor out \(p\):

$$
p\left(q^2s_{jj}-\mu\right)=0
$$

Ignoring the trivial solution \(p=0\):

$$
q^2s_{jj}=\mu
$$

Solving for \(q\):

$$
q=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{s_{jj}}}
$$

---

# 12. Mutation-Selection Balance

The equilibrium frequency of a rare recessive deleterious allele is therefore

$$
\boxed{q=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{s_{jj}}}}
$$

This is the classic mutation-selection balance equation.

It predicts that deleterious alleles persist because mutation continually introduces them while selection continually removes them.

---

# 13. Biological Interpretation

The mutation-selection balance equation reveals two important relationships.

## Effect of Mutation Rate

As mutation rate increases:

$$
\mu \uparrow
\Rightarrow
q \uparrow
$$

More deleterious alleles are introduced each generation.

Therefore equilibrium frequency increases.

---

## Effect of Selection Strength

As selection becomes stronger:

$$
s_{jj} \uparrow
\Rightarrow
q \downarrow
$$

Selection removes deleterious alleles more efficiently.

Therefore equilibrium frequency decreases.

---

# Example

Suppose

$$
\mu = 10^{-6}
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=0.01
$$

Then

$$
q=
\sqrt{
\frac{10^{-6}}
{0.01}
}
$$

$$
q=\sqrt{10^{-4}}
$$

$$
q=0.01
$$

The deleterious allele persists at approximately 1% frequency despite ongoing selection against it.

---

# Key Take Home Messages

1. Mutation continuously introduces new genetic variation.

2. Mutation alone produces an equilibrium:

$$
\hat p=\frac{\nu}{\mu+\nu}
$$

3. Most non-neutral mutations are deleterious.
4. Purifying selection removes deleterious alleles from populations.
5. Mutation and purifying selection act in opposite directions.
6. Their interaction produces mutation-selection balance.
7. For a rare recessive deleterious allele:

$$
q=\sqrt{\frac{\mu}{s_{jj}}}
$$

8. Deleterious alleles persist because mutation continually reintroduces them even as selection removes them.

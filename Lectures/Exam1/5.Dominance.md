# Topic 5: Dominance

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, students should be able to:

- Define dominance in a population genetic context.
- Explain how dominance modifies the action of natural selection on heterozygotes.
- Calculate the dominance coefficient, $h$.
- Derive genotype fitnesses from selection coefficients and dominance relationships.
- Predict how dominance affects the rate and long term fate of an allele.
- Derive the selection equation when dominance is present.

---

# 1. Revisiting the Selection Equation

Recall the general equation describing the rate of allele frequency change under selection:

$$
\Delta p =
\frac{pq\Big[p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})\Big]}
{\bar w}
$$

This equation tells us that the rate of evolutionary change depends on:

- The frequency of the alleles in the population.

- The fitness differences among genotypes carrying those alleles.

In the previous lecture we examined a special case where:

$$
w_{ii}=w_{ij}
$$

Under this scenario, allele $i$ behaves as a completely dominant allele.

But what happens when:

$$
w_{ii}\neq w_{ij}
$$

To answer that question, we introduce the concept of dominance.

---

# 2. What is Dominance in Population Genetics?

In classical Mendelian genetics, dominance refers to phenotype.

For example:

- Purple flowers dominate white flowers.
- Brown eyes dominate blue eyes.

Population geneticists are interested in something different.

Dominance measures:

> How strongly selection acts on the heterozygote relative to the two homozygotes.

Therefore, dominance is fundamentally a fitness concept.

It describes where the heterozygote falls between the two homozygotes in terms of fitness.

---

# 3. A Coral Example

Suppose the absolute fitnesses are:

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|----------|
| ii | 1000 |
| ij | 1000 |
| jj | 800 |

Converting to relative fitness:

| Genotype | Relative Fitness |
|-----------|------------------|
| ii | 1.0 |
| ij | 1.0 |
| jj | 0.8 |

The corresponding selection coefficients are:

$$
w = 1-s
$$

Therefore:

$$
s_{ii}=0
$$

$$
s_{ij}=0
$$

$$
s_{jj}=0.2
$$

Notice that:

$$
w_{ii}=w_{ij}
$$

The heterozygote has exactly the same fitness as genotype $ii$.

Thus allele $i$ is completely dominant.

---

# 4. What if the Heterozygote is Intermediate?

Suppose instead that:

| Genotype | Relative Fitness |
|-----------|------------------|
| ii | 1.0 |
| ij | 0.9 |
| jj | 0.8 |

The heterozygote no longer matches either homozygote.

This suggests a dominance interaction.

We define the heterozygote fitness as:

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

where:

- $s_{jj}$ is the selection coefficient of genotype $jj$
- $h$ is the dominance coefficient

Dominance therefore modifies how much of the fitness effect of allele $j$ is expressed in heterozygotes.

---

# 5. Solving for the Dominance Coefficient

Given:

$$
w_{ij}=0.9
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=0.2
$$

Substitute into

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

to obtain:

$$
0.9=1-0.2h
$$

Subtract 1:

$$
-0.1=-0.2h
$$

Divide both sides by $-0.2$:

$$
h=0.5
$$

Thus the heterozygote lies exactly halfway between the two homozygotes.

---

# 6. General Formula for Dominance

More generally:

$$
h=
\frac{w_{ii}-w_{ij}}
{w_{ii}-w_{jj}}
$$

Interpretation:

- $h=0$ means complete dominance of allele $i$
- $h=1$ means complete recessivity of allele $i$
- $h=0.5$ means additive effects
- Intermediate values describe partial dominance

---

# 7. Special Cases of Dominance

## Complete Dominance

$$
h=0
$$

Then:

$$
w_{ij}=w_{ii}
$$

The heterozygote is indistinguishable from genotype $ii$.

---

## Additivity

$$
h=0.5
$$

Then the heterozygote lies exactly midway between the homozygotes.

---

## Complete Recessivity

$$
h=1
$$

Then:

$$
w_{ij}=w_{jj}
$$

The advantageous effects of allele $i$ are completely hidden in heterozygotes.

---

# 8. Selection with Dominance

Under directional selection:

$$
w_{ii}=1
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s_{jj}
$$

Substituting these expressions into the standard recursion equation gives:
Starting from the standard viability selection recursion,

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{
p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}
}
{\bar w}
$$

where

$$
\bar w=
p^2w_{ii}
+
2pqw_{ij}
+
q^2w_{jj}
$$

Substitute the directional selection fitnesses:

$$
w_{ii}=1
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s_{jj}
$$

The numerator becomes:

$$
p^2+pq(1-hs_{jj})
$$

Now substitute into the denominator:

$$
\bar w=
p^2(1)
+
2pq(1-hs_{jj})
+
q^2(1-s_{jj})
$$

Expand:

$$
\bar w=p^2+2pq-2pqhs_{jj}+q^2-q^2s_{jj}
$$

Use

$$
p^2+2pq+q^2=1
$$

to simplify:

$$
\bar w=1-2pqhs_{jj}-q^2s_{jj}
$$

Substitute the simplified numerator and denominator back into the recursion equation:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{
p^2+p q(1-hs_{jj})}{1-2pqhs_{jj}-q^2s_{jj}
}
$$

This version of the equation is useful because it describes evolution using a single selection coefficient:

$$
s_{jj}
$$

and a single dominance coefficient:

$$
h
$$

---

# 9. The Special Case of Complete Dominance

When:

$$
h=0
$$

the recursion equation becomes:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2+pq}
{1-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

This is the same dominance model encountered previously when the heterozygote and favored homozygote have identical fitness.

---

# 10. Deriving the Rate of Selection with Dominance

Begin with the standard selection equation:

$$
\Delta p =
\frac{
pq\Big[
p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+
q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})
\Big]}
{\bar w}
$$

Assume:

$$
w_{ii}=1
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s_{jj}
$$

Substituting:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{pq\Big[p(1-(1-hs_{jj}))+
q((1-hs_{jj})-(1-s_{jj}))
\Big]}
{\bar w}
$$

Simplifying:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{
pq
\Big[phs_{jj}+
q(s_{jj}-hs_{jj})
\Big]}
{\bar w}
$$

Factor out $s_{jj}$:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
pqs_{jj}
\Big[
ph+q(1-h)
\Big]
}{\bar w}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
pqs_{jj}
\Big[
ph+q(1-h)
\Big]}{1-2pqhs_{jj}-q^2s_{jj}
}
$$

This is the directional selection equation including dominance.

---

# 11. Why Dominance Matters

Dominance affects whether selection can "see" an allele.

The key principle is:

> Selection acts on phenotypes and therefore on genotypes, not directly on alleles.

As a result, heterozygotes can either expose or hide the effects of an allele.

---

# 12. Recessive Alleles Can Hide from Selection

When an advantageous allele is recessive:

$$
h \approx 1
$$

most copies initially occur in heterozygotes.

Because heterozygotes do not express the full fitness advantage, selection cannot efficiently increase the allele's frequency.

Evolution proceeds slowly.

Only after enough homozygotes appear can selection act strongly.

---

# 13. Dominance Alters the Long Term Fate of Alleles

A fully recessive deleterious allele can persist in populations because it remains hidden inside heterozygotes.

Many human genetic disorders illustrate this principle.

Examples include:

- Cystic fibrosis
- Phenylketonuria
- Haemochromatosis
- Spinal muscular atrophy

Because carriers are heterozygous, selection cannot completely eliminate these alleles.

This phenomenon is sometimes called genetic hiding.

---

# 14. Dominance Alters the Rate of Selection

Dominance influences not only whether an allele persists, but also how quickly evolution occurs.

When an advantageous allele is dominant:

- Selection acts immediately.
- Allele frequency rises rapidly when rare.

When an advantageous allele is recessive:

- Selection is initially weak.
- The allele remains hidden in heterozygotes.
- Evolution is initially slow.

Thus both the magnitude of selection and the dominance coefficient determine evolutionary rate.

---

# Key Take Home Messages

1. Dominance modifies how selection acts on the heterozygote.

2. The dominance coefficient is defined as:

$$
h=\frac{w_{ii}-w_{ij}}{w_{ii}-w_{jj}}
$$

3. Heterozygote fitness can be written as:

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs_{jj}
$$

4. Complete dominance occurs when:

$$
h=0
$$

5. Complete recessivity occurs when:

$$
h=1
$$

6. The rate of selection with dominance is:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{pqs_{jj}[ph+q(1-h)]}{1-2pqhs_{jj}-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

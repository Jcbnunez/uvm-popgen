# Topic 4: The Rate of Natural Selection

## Previously in Population Genetics

### Under Hardy-Weinberg

Given:

$$
p = 0.60
$$

Genotype frequencies:

$$
x_{ii} = 0.36
$$

$$
x_{ij} = 0.48
$$

No change occurs from generation \(t\) to generation \(t+1\):

$$
x_{ii}(t+1) = 0.36
$$

$$
x_{ij}(t+1) = 0.48
$$

### Under Directional Selection

Initial genotype frequencies:

$$
x_{ii} = 0.16
$$

After selection:

$$
x_{ii}(t+1) = 0.1936
$$

$$
x_{ij}(t+1) = 0.4958
$$

Relative to the Hardy-Weinberg expectation:

* Some genotypes are selected for.

* Some genotypes are selected against.

---

# Visualizing Allele Frequency Change

Suppose:

$$
p_t = 0.60
$$

After a change in the environment (for example, an increase in temperature),

$$
p_{t+1} = 0.61
$$

Under Hardy-Weinberg:

$$
\Delta p = 0
$$

Under directional selection:

$$
\Delta p > 0
$$

where

$$
\Delta p = p_{t+1} - p_t
$$

Our goal is to derive a general expression for:

$$
\Delta p
$$

---

# Deriving the Rate of Selection

Recall:

$$
\Delta p = p_{t+1} - p
$$

and

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{p^2 w_{ii} + pqw_{ij}}{\bar w}
$$

Substituting:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p^2 w_{ii} + pqw_{ij}}{\bar w}-p
$$

Multiply by a common denominator:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p^2 w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}-p\bar w}{\bar w}
$$

Recall mean fitness:

$$
\bar w=p^2w_{ii}+2pqw_{ij}+q^2w_{jj}
$$

Substituting:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}-p(p^2w_{ii}+2pqw_{ij}+q^2w_{jj})}{\bar w}
$$

Expanding:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}-p^3w_{ii}-2p^2qw_{ij}-pq^2w_{jj}}{\bar w}
$$

---

# Factoring Out p

Notice every term contains \(p\):

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p\left(pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}-p^2w_{ii}-2pqw_{ij}-q^2w_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Rearrange:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p\left(pw_{ii}-p^2w_{ii}+qw_{ij}-2pqw_{ij}-q^2w_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

---

# Simplifying Terms

Recall:

$$
q = 1 - p
$$

Therefore:

$$
p - p^2=p(1-p)=pq
$$

Substituting:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p\left(pqw_{ii}+qw_{ij}-2pqw_{ij}-q^2w_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Now simplify:

$$
qw_{ij}-2pqw_{ij}=qw_{ij}(1-2p)
$$

giving

$$
\Delta p=\frac{p\left(pqw_{ii}+qw_{ij}(1-2p)-q^2w_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Factor out \(q\):

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq\left(pw_{ii}+w_{ij}(1-2p)-qw_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Since

$$
1-2p = q-p
$$

we obtain

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq\left(pw_{ii}+w_{ij}(q-p)-qw_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Expanding:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq\left(pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}-pw_{ij}-qw_{jj}\right)}{\bar w}
$$

Collecting terms:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq\left[p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})\right]}
{\bar w}
$$

---

# Fundamental Equation for the Rate of Natural Selection

$$
\boxed{\Delta p=\frac{pq\left[p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})\right]}{\bar w}}
$$

---

# Biological Interpretation

This equation reveals two fundamental properties of natural selection.

## 1. Selection Depends on Genetic Variation

The factor

$$
pq
$$

measures the amount of genetic variation in the population.

Selection is fastest when (assuming all variation is fitness related!):

$$
p=q=0.5
$$

because

$$
pq = 0.25
$$

which is the maximum possible value.

When an allele is rare:

$$
p \approx 0
$$

or

$$
p \approx 1
$$

then

$$
pq \approx 0
$$

and selection proceeds slowly.

---

## 2. Selection Depends on Fitness Differences

The second term

$$
p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})
$$

captures fitness differences among genotypes.

The larger the fitness differences, the stronger and faster natural selection acts.

---

# Adaptation From New Mutations

A beneficial mutation first enters the population at very low frequency.

### Phase 1: Mutation is Rare

Since

$$
pq
$$

is small,

selection is slow.

### Phase 2: Mutation Becomes Common

As allele frequencies approach intermediate values,

$$
pq
$$

increases.

Selection accelerates and "snowballs."

### Phase 3: Mutation Nears Fixation

As one allele approaches fixation,

$$
pq \rightarrow 0
$$

again.

Selection slows once more.

---

# General Principle

The trajectory of adaptation is typically:

1. Slow at the beginning.

2. Fast in the middle.

3. Slow near fixation.

The overall speed also depends on the strength of selection, often represented by:

$$
s
$$

where larger values of \(s\) produce faster evolutionary change.

---

# Novel Mutations and Resistance Evolution

Novel mutations play a central role in:

* Antibiotic resistance
* Pesticide resistance

A beneficial mutation can spread rapidly once it becomes common enough, but the initial spread is often slow because the mutation begins at low frequency.

---

# Key Take Home Messages

1. The rate of selection is:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq\left[p(w_{ii}-w_{ij})+q(w_{ij}-w_{jj})\right]}{\bar w}
$$

2. Selection is fastest when genetic variation is highest.

3. Selection is slow when alleles are very rare or nearly fixed.

4. Adaptation from new mutations is often slow initially.

5. Rapid adaptation frequently occurs through standing genetic variation already present in populations.

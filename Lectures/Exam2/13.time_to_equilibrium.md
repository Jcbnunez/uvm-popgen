# Time to Equilibrium Under Balancing Selection

---

# Learning Goals

By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

1. Explain why balancing selection approaches an equilibrium rather than fixation.
2. Calculate the equilibrium frequency under true overdominance.
3. Construct an equilibrium odds ratio.
4. Generalize the time-to-fixation framework to systems with internal equilibria.
5. Estimate the rate at which populations approach equilibrium.
6. Explain several ways to estimate the equilibrium rate constant $k$.
7. Use logarithms and linear equations to estimate time to equilibrium.
8. Explain why equilibrium is never reached exactly in a finite number of generations.

---

# 1. A New Evolutionary Question

In our directional-selection lectures, we focused on a simple question:

> How many generations are required for an advantageous allele to reach fixation?

To answer that question, we transformed allele frequencies into odds:

$$
Odds = \frac{p}{q}
$$

where

$$
q=1-p.
$$

As fixation is approached,

$$
p\rightarrow1
$$

and

$$
q\rightarrow0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{p}{q}\rightarrow\infty.
$$

This observation allowed us to derive the familiar time-to-fixation equation:

$$
t=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\right)}{\ln(1+s)}.
$$

Today we ask a related but fundamentally different question:

> How many generations are required for a population to approach a stable equilibrium?

---

# 2. Why Balancing Selection Is Different

Under directional selection, one allele is consistently favored and the population moves toward fixation.

Under balancing selection, both alleles are maintained and the population evolves toward an internal equilibrium.

Consider a true overdominance model:

$$
w_{ii}=0.8,\quad w_{ij}=1.0,\quad w_{jj}=0.2
$$

The corresponding selection coefficients are:

$$
s_{ii}=0.2,\quad s_{jj}=0.8
$$

The equilibrium frequency is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

Substituting:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{0.8}{0.2+0.8}=0.8
$$

Therefore:

$$
q_{eq}=1-p_{eq}=0.2
$$

Notice the key difference:

| Evolutionary Process | Equilibrium |
|----------|-----------|
| Directional Selection | $p=1$ |
| True Overdominance | $p=0.8$ |

The population is not moving toward fixation.

Instead, it is moving toward an internal equilibrium.

---

# 3. The Key Insight

To generalize the fixation-time framework, we first need to understand what the odds ratio was actually measuring.

Recall:

$$
\frac{p}{q}=\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

Notice that the denominator,

$$
1-p,
$$

is simply the remaining distance to fixation.

Since fixation occurs at:

$$
p_{eq}=1,
$$

the fixation odds can be interpreted as:

$$
\frac{\text{current position}}{\text{remaining distance to equilibrium}}
$$

This interpretation is much more general than fixation itself.

If we can identify the remaining distance to any equilibrium, then we can construct an odds ratio for that equilibrium.

---

# 4. Generalizing the Odds Ratio

Suppose our balancing-selection equilibrium is:

$$
p_{eq}=0.8
$$

The remaining distance to equilibrium is:

$$
p_{eq}-p
$$

Following exactly the same logic used for fixation, we define the equilibrium odds:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{p_{eq}-p}
$$

Interpretation:

- Numerator = where the population currently is.
- Denominator = how far the population still is from equilibrium.

Thus:

$$
O_E=\frac{\text{current position}}{\text{remaining distance}}
$$

---

# 5. Why This Works

Consider several allele frequencies:

| p | Equilibrium Odds |
|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.20/(0.80−0.20) = 0.33 |
| 0.60 | 0.60/(0.80−0.20) = 3.00 |
| 0.79 | 0.79/(0.80−0.01) = 79.0 |

Notice what happens as:

$$
p\rightarrow p_{eq}
$$

The remaining distance:

$$
p_{eq}-p
$$

approaches zero.

Therefore:

$$
O_E\rightarrow\infty
$$

This is exactly the behavior we observed when a population approached fixation.

The equilibrium odds become larger and larger as the population gets closer and closer to equilibrium.

---

# 6. Example Simulation

Suppose a deterministic simulation produces the following allele frequencies:

| Generation | p |
|------------|------|
| 0 | 0.200 |
| 10 | 0.632 |
| 20 | 0.785 |

Given:

$$
p_{eq}=0.8
$$

the equilibrium odds are:

| Generation | $O_E$ |
|------------|-------|
| 0 | 0.333 |
| 10 | 3.76 |
| 20 | 52.33 |

Notice that the odds increase very slowly at first and then increase rapidly as equilibrium is approached.

---

# 7. Logarithms Straighten the Curve

Unfortunately, raw odds do not change linearly through time.

This makes prediction difficult.

We encountered exactly the same problem when studying fixation.

The solution was to take logarithms.

Calculate:

$$
\ln(O_E)
$$

| Generation | $O_E$ | $\ln(O_E)$ |
|------------|-------|-----------|
| 0 | 0.333 | -1.10 |
| 10 | 3.76 | 1.32 |
| 20 | 52.33 | 3.96 |

Notice that the logarithms now fall approximately along a straight line.

Once a curved process becomes approximately linear, we can use simple algebra to describe it.

---

# 8. Estimating the Rate of Approach

Recall the general equation for a straight line:

$$
y=a+bx
$$

where:

- $a$ = intercept
- $b$ = slope
- $x$ = independent variable
- $y$ = response variable

For our evolutionary system:

$$
y=\ln(O_E)
$$

and

$$
x=t.
$$

The slope therefore tells us:

> How rapidly is the population approaching equilibrium?

Using generations 0 and 20:

$$
k=\frac{\ln(O_{E,20})-\ln(O_{E,0})}{20}
$$

Substituting:

$$
k=\frac{3.96-(-1.10)}{20}
$$

$$
k=\frac{5.06}{20}
$$

$$
k=0.253
$$

---

# 9. What Does k Mean?

The parameter

$$
k=0.253
$$

is the average increase in log-equilibrium-odds per generation.

Biologically:

- Large $k$ → equilibrium is reached rapidly.
- Small $k$ → equilibrium is reached slowly.

A useful way to think about this is:

> The equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends, whereas $k$ tells us how quickly it gets there.

These are two different biological properties of the system.

---

# 10. Building the Time-to-Equilibrium Equation

Once we know the slope, we can write the linear equation:

$$
\ln(O_E)=a+kt
$$

The intercept equals the initial value:

$$
a=\ln(0.333)=-1.10
$$

Therefore:

$$
\ln(O_E)=-1.10+0.253t
$$

This equation allows us to estimate future states of the population.

---

# 11. A Critical Question: Where Does k Come From?

At this point you should be wondering:

> If I know the selection coefficients and starting allele frequency, can I calculate k directly?

Usually, no.

The equilibrium frequency:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

is determined directly by the balancing-selection model.

However, $k$ describes the trajectory toward that equilibrium.

In practice, $k$ usually must be estimated.

This distinction is important:

- Equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends.
- $k$ tells us how quickly evolution gets there.

---

# 12. Estimating k

There are several ways to estimate $k$.

### Method 1: Two Time Points

Calculate equilibrium odds at two generations and compute:

$$
k=\frac{\ln(O_{E,2})-\ln(O_{E,1})}{t_2-t_1}
$$

This is simply the slope formula from algebra.

---

### Method 2: Many Time Points

A better approach is to calculate $\ln(O_E)$ for many generations and fit:

$$
\ln(O_E)=a+kt
$$

using linear regression.

Advantages:

- Uses all available information.
- Less sensitive to noise.
- Produces a more stable estimate.

---

### Method 3: Real Data

Suppose field biologists measure allele frequencies through time.

If the equilibrium frequency is known:

1. Calculate equilibrium odds.
2. Take logarithms.
3. Estimate the slope.

In this way, $k$ can be measured directly from natural populations.

---

# 13. Defining "Close Enough"

A subtle problem now appears.

True equilibrium means:

$$
p=0.800000000\ldots
$$

Exactly reaching equilibrium would require:

$$
p_{eq}-p=0
$$

which would produce:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{0}
$$

and therefore:

$$
O_E=\infty
$$

No finite number of generations can produce an infinite odds ratio.

Consequently:

> Equilibrium is never reached exactly.

Instead, we must define what counts as "close enough."

---

# 14. A Practical Definition

Suppose we define equilibrium as being within:

$$
0.001
$$

of the true equilibrium.

This corresponds to:

$$
p=0.799
$$

The remaining distance is:

$$
0.8-0.799=0.001
$$

The equilibrium odds become:

$$
O_E=\frac{0.799}{0.001}=799
$$

Taking logarithms:

$$
\ln(799)=6.68
$$

This gives us a target value.

---

# 15. Solving for Time

Recall:

$$
\ln(O_E)=-1.10+0.253t
$$

Substitute the target:

$$
6.68=-1.10+0.253t
$$

Solve:

$$
7.78=0.253t
$$

$$
t=30.7
$$

Therefore:

$$
t\approx31\text{ generations}
$$

This is our estimate of the time required to get within 0.001 of equilibrium.

---

# 16. What Happens If We Simulate Forever?

Students often wonder:

> Could the population overshoot the equilibrium?

The answer is no.

Recall the overdominance rate equation:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

The sign of evolutionary change depends on:

$$
qs_{jj}-ps_{ii}
$$

If:

$$
p < p_{eq}
$$

then:

$$
\Delta p>0
$$

and selection increases $p$.

If:

$$
p > p_{eq}
$$

then:

$$
\Delta p<0
$$

and selection decreases $p$.

Thus equilibrium acts as a stable evolutionary attractor.

---

# 17. Simulating 1000 Generations

A long-term simulation might look like:

| Generation | p |
|------------|---------|
| 20 | 0.785 |
| 50 | 0.7998 |
| 100 | 0.799999 |
| 500 | 0.800000 |
| 1000 | 0.800000 |

The population continually approaches equilibrium.

It never permanently overshoots equilibrium.

It never reaches equilibrium exactly.

Instead, the remaining distance simply becomes smaller and smaller through time.

---

# 18. Biological Interpretation

At the start of the simulation:

$$
p=0.2
$$

The population is far from equilibrium.

As a result, selection acts strongly.

As the population approaches:

$$
p=0.8
$$

the selective imbalance becomes smaller.

Eventually:

$$
qs_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

and therefore:

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

The evolutionary "pull" toward equilibrium weakens as the population gets closer to its destination.

Consequently:

- Early evolution is rapid.
- Late evolution is slow.
- The final approach to equilibrium takes many generations.

This behavior is common to most stable evolutionary equilibria.

---

# Connection to Time-to-Fixation

Notice the parallels.

| Directional Selection | Balancing Selection |
|----------------------|---------------------|
| $p_{eq}=1$ | $p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}$ |
| $\frac{p}{1-p}$ | $\frac{p}{p_{eq}-p}$ |
| fixation | internal equilibrium |
| time-to-fixation | time-to-equilibrium |

The mathematics is nearly identical.

Fixation is simply a special case of an equilibrium where:

$$
p_{eq}=1
$$

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

1. True overdominance evolves toward an internal equilibrium rather than fixation.

2. The equilibrium frequency is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

3. The equilibrium odds ratio is:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{p_{eq}-p}
$$

4. As equilibrium is approached:

$$
O_E\rightarrow\infty
$$

5. Logarithms transform the curved approach to equilibrium into an approximately linear relationship.

6. The parameter $k$ measures the rate at which equilibrium is approached.

7. The equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends; $k$ tells us how quickly it gets there.

8. $k$ is usually estimated from simulations, regression, or empirical data.

9. Equilibrium is never reached exactly in a deterministic model.

10. Time to equilibrium therefore means time to become sufficiently close to equilibrium.

11. The same odds-ratio logic used for time-to-fixation can be generalized to estimate time-to-equilibrium.

# Time to Equilibrium Under Balancing Selection


# Learning Goals

2. Calculate the equilibrium frequency under balancing selection.
3. Describe how distance from equilibrium changes through time.

5. Estimate the time required to approach equilibrium.

# Important Note

The time-to-equilibrium framework developed here should be viewed as an approximation.

The exact behavior of balancing-selection models can be analyzed using calculus and local stability analysis. Those methods are beyond the scope of BIOL 4260.

Instead, we will use algebra and recursion models to derive an intuitive approximation for the rate at which populations approach equilibrium.

Unlike the empirical $k$ approach, the method developed here is tied directly to the underlying population-genetic model and therefore has a stronger biological interpretation.

---

# 1. A Reminder: Time to Fixation

Earlier in the semester we asked:

> How many generations are required for an advantageous allele to reach fixation?

Under a simple additive directional-selection model ($h=0.5$), the fixation process can often be approximated using the log-odds framework.

The odds ratio is:

$$
\frac{p}{q}
$$

where

$$
q=1-p.
$$

As fixation is approached:

$$
p\rightarrow1
$$

and

$$
q\rightarrow0.
$$

Thus:

$$
\frac{p}{q}\rightarrow\infty.
$$

This led to the time-to-fixation equation:

$$
t=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\right)}{\ln(1+s)}.
$$

Importantly, this approach works best under additive directional selection ($h=0.5$), where the change in log-odds is approximately linear through time.

Today we ask a different question:

> How many generations are required to approach an internal equilibrium?

---

# 2. True Overdominance Produces an Internal Equilibrium

Suppose fitnesses are:

$$
w_{ii}=0.8,\quad w_{ij}=1.0,\quad w_{jj}=0.2
$$

The corresponding selection coefficients are:

$$
s_{ii}=0.2,\quad s_{jj}=0.8
$$

Recall that under true overdominance:

$$
w_{ij}>w_{ii}
$$

and

$$
w_{ij}>w_{jj}.
$$

The equilibrium frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

Substituting:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{0.8}{0.2+0.8}=0.8
$$

Thus:

$$
q_{eq}=0.2.
$$

Unlike directional selection, the population does not move toward fixation.

Instead it moves toward:

$$
p=0.8.
$$

---

# 3. The Core Question

Suppose the population begins at:

$$
p_0=0.2.
$$

Eventually it will approach:

$$
p_{eq}=0.8.
$$

But how long will that take?

To answer this question we need to understand how rapidly the distance from equilibrium shrinks through time.

Define the distance from equilibrium as:

$$
d_t=|p_{eq}-p_t|.
$$

This quantity tells us how far the population still is from equilibrium at generation $t$.

---

# 4. A Key Property of Stable Equilibria

When the population is very near equilibrium, the dynamics can be approximated by simple geometric decay.

Specifically:

$$
d_{t+1}\approx \lambda d_t
$$

where:

$$
0<\lambda<1.
$$

The parameter $\lambda$ is called the asymptotic decay factor. 

Interpretation:

- $\lambda=0$ means instantaneous convergence.
- $\lambda=1$ means no convergence.
- Intermediate values describe gradual convergence.

Thus:

> The distance to equilibrium shrinks by the same fraction each generation.

This is analogous to exponential decay.

---

# 5. Introducing the Decay Factor

For true overdominance, local stability analysis gives:

$$
\lambda=1-\frac{s_{ii}s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}-s_{ii}s_{jj}}.
$$

Notice that this equation depends directly on the selection coefficients.

Unlike the empirical parameter $k$, the decay factor is derived directly from the evolutionary model. The exact derivation of this parameter needs calculus, so we won't cover it in detail here.

---

# 6. Calculate λ for Our Example

Recall:

$$
s_{ii}=0.2
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=0.8.
$$

Substituting:

$$
\lambda=1-\frac{(0.2)(0.8)}{0.2+0.8-(0.2)(0.8)}
$$

Compute the numerator:

$$
(0.2)(0.8)=0.16
$$

Compute the denominator:

$$
0.2+0.8-0.16=0.84
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lambda=1-\frac{0.16}{0.84}
$$

$$
\lambda=1-0.1905
$$

$$
\lambda=0.8095.
$$

Thus each generation removes approximately:

$$
1-0.8095=0.1905
$$

or about

$$
19\%
$$

of the remaining distance to equilibrium.

---

# 7. Distance Through Time

Because the distance shrinks geometrically:

$$
d_t=d_0\lambda^t.
$$

This is directly analogous to the recursion:

$$
d_{t+1}=\lambda d_t.
$$

Starting from the initial distance:

$$
d_0=|p_{eq}-p_0|
$$

we obtain:

$$
d_0=|0.8-0.2|
$$

$$
d_0=0.6.
$$

Therefore:

$$
d_t=0.6(0.8095)^t.
$$

This equation predicts the remaining distance from equilibrium after $t$ generations.

---

# 8. Solving for Time

Suppose we wish to know how long it takes to reach some target frequency.

Let:

$$
p_t
$$

be the frequency we wish to reach.

The corresponding distance is:

$$
d_t=|p_{eq}-p_t|.
$$

Using:

$$
d_t=d_0\lambda^t
$$

divide both sides by $d_0$:

$$
\frac{d_t}{d_0}=\lambda^t.
$$

Taking logarithms:

$$
\ln\left(\frac{d_t}{d_0}\right)=t\ln(\lambda).
$$

Solving for time:

$$
t=\frac{\ln(d_t/d_0)}{\ln(\lambda)}.
$$

Rearranging slightly:

$$
t\approx\frac{\ln\left(\frac{|p_{eq}-p_0|}{|p_{eq}-p_t|}\right)}{-\ln(\lambda)}.
$$

This is our time-to-equilibrium equation.

---

# 9. Example Calculation

Suppose:

$$
p_0=0.2
$$

and we define "effectively at equilibrium" as:

$$
p_t=0.799.
$$

Then:

$$
d_0=|0.8-0.2|=0.6
$$

and

$$
d_t=|0.8-0.799|=0.001.
$$

Using:

$$
\lambda=0.8095
$$

substitute into the equation:

$$
t=\frac{\ln(0.6/0.001)}{-\ln(0.8095)}.
$$

Compute the numerator:

$$
\ln(600)=6.397.
$$

Compute the denominator:

$$
-\ln(0.8095)=0.211.
$$

Therefore:

$$
t=\frac{6.397}{0.211}.
$$

Thus:

$$
t\approx30.3\text{ generations}.
$$

So we estimate:

$$
t\approx30\text{ generations}.
$$

to get within 0.001 of equilibrium. Warning: This number is an approximation because the decay factor $lambda$ is strictly accurate only when the population is very close to equilibrium. When a population starts far from equilibrium, the actual speed of evolution depends on genetic variation $pq$. If an allele starts at a very low frequency (e.g., $p_0 = 0.001$), initial change is actually very slow because the allele is too rare for selection to act on efficiently; it then accelerates through intermediate frequencies before slowing down again as it approaches $p_{eq}$. Computer simulations are the best way to observe these exact dynamics without using calculus.


---

# 10. Why Equilibrium Is Never Reached Exactly

Exact equilibrium would require:

$$
|p_{eq}-p|=0.
$$

Substituting into the time equation gives:

$$
\ln(0).
$$

But:

$$
\ln(0)
$$

is undefined.

Therefore:

> Exact equilibrium requires an infinite number of generations.

This is the same phenomenon we encountered when studying fixation.

In deterministic models:

- fixation is never reached exactly,
- equilibrium is never reached exactly.

Instead, we define a biologically reasonable threshold and ask when the population gets sufficiently close.

---

# 11. Biological Interpretation

Initially the population is far from equilibrium:

$$
p_0=0.2.
$$

The selective imbalance is large.

Consequently, selection changes allele frequencies rapidly.

As the population approaches equilibrium:

$$
qs_{jj}-ps_{ii}
$$

becomes smaller.

Eventually:

$$
qs_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

and:

$$
\Delta p=0.
$$

The evolutionary pull becomes progressively weaker as the population gets closer to equilibrium.

Consequently:

- Early evolution is rapid.
- Late evolution is slow.
- The final approach to equilibrium takes many generations.

This behavior is common to most stable evolutionary equilibria.

---

# Connection to Time-to-Fixation

Notice the similarities.

| Directional Selection | Balancing Selection |
|---|---|
| Additive directional-selection model | True overdominance |
| Fixation equilibrium | Internal equilibrium |
| Log-odds framework | Distance-decay framework |
| Time-to-fixation | Time-to-equilibrium |

For directional selection:

$$
p_{eq}=1.
$$

For balancing selection:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

In both cases:

1. Identify the equilibrium.
2. Measure the remaining distance.
3. Determine how rapidly the distance shrinks.
4. Solve for the time required to reach a chosen threshold.

Thus the two problems are conceptually very similar even though their mathematical details differ.

# Key Take-Home Messages

1. True overdominance produces a stable internal equilibrium.

2. The equilibrium frequency is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

3. The distance from equilibrium is:

$$
d_t=|p_{eq}-p_t|.
$$

4. Near equilibrium, distance decays geometrically:

$$
d_{t+1}=\lambda d_t.
$$

5. The asymptotic decay factor is:

$$
\lambda=1-\frac{s_{ii}s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}-s_{ii}s_{jj}}.
$$

6. Distance through time follows:

$$
d_t=d_0\lambda^t.
$$

7. Time to equilibrium is approximated by:

$$
t\approx\frac{\ln\left(\frac{|p_{eq}-p_0|}{|p_{eq}-p_t|}\right)}{-\ln(\lambda)}.
$$

8. Equilibrium is never reached exactly in a deterministic model.

9. Time-to-equilibrium therefore means time to become sufficiently close to equilibrium.

10. Unlike the empirical $k$ approach, the decay-factor method is tied directly to the underlying balancing-selection model and therefore has a clearer biological interpretation.

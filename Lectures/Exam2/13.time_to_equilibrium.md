# Important Note

The approach developed in this lecture is a heuristic approximation rather than an exact population-genetic solution.

The exact rate at which populations approach equilibrium under balancing selection can be derived using calculus and local stability analysis. Those methods are beyond the scope of BIOL 4260.

Instead, we will develop a simple algebraic framework that approximates the time required to approach equilibrium. The goal is not to obtain a perfectly exact answer, but rather to develop intuition for evolutionary timescales.

Similarly, recall that the time-to-fixation equation from our directional-selection lectures was derived under a particular additive directional-selection model. The framework developed here borrows the same logic and adapts it to populations evolving toward an internal equilibrium.

---

# 1. A New Evolutionary Question

In our directional-selection lectures we asked:

> How many generations are required for an advantageous allele to reach fixation?

To answer that question, we transformed allele frequencies into odds:

$$
\frac{p}{q}
$$

where

$$
q=1-p.
$$

As fixation is approached,

$$
p\rightarrow1
$$

and therefore

$$
q\rightarrow0.
$$

Consequently,

$$
\frac{p}{q}\rightarrow\infty.
$$

This observation allowed us to derive the familiar time-to-fixation equation:

$$
t=\frac{\ln\left(\frac{p_1(1-p_0)}{p_0(1-p_1)}\right)}{\ln(1+s)}.
$$

Today we ask a related question:

> How many generations are required for a population to approach a stable equilibrium?

---

# 2. Why Balancing Selection Is Different

Under directional selection, one allele is consistently favored and the population moves toward fixation.

Under balancing selection, multiple alleles are maintained and the population moves toward a stable internal equilibrium.

Consider a true overdominance model:

$$
w_{ii}=0.8,\quad w_{ij}=1.0,\quad w_{jj}=0.2
$$

The corresponding selection coefficients are:

$$
s_{ii}=0.2,\quad s_{jj}=0.8
$$

The equilibrium frequency is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

Substituting:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{0.8}{0.2+0.8}=0.8
$$

Therefore:

$$
q_{eq}=1-p_{eq}=0.2
$$

Notice the key difference:

| Evolutionary Process | Equilibrium |
|---|---|
| Directional Selection | $p_{eq}=1$ |
| True Overdominance | $p_{eq}=0.8$ |

The population is not moving toward fixation.

Instead, it is moving toward an internal equilibrium.

---

# 3. The Key Insight

To generalize the fixation framework, we first need to understand what the odds ratio was actually measuring.

Recall:

$$
\frac{p}{q}=\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

Notice that the denominator,

$$
1-p,
$$

is simply the remaining distance to fixation.

Because fixation occurs when:

$$
p_{eq}=1,
$$

the fixation odds can be interpreted as:

$$
\frac{\text{current position}}{\text{distance to equilibrium}}
$$

This interpretation is more important than the specific equation itself.

If we can identify the distance to any equilibrium, then we can construct an odds ratio for that equilibrium.

---

# 4. Generalizing the Odds Ratio

Suppose the equilibrium occurs at:

$$
p_{eq}=0.8
$$

The distance to equilibrium is:

$$
|p_{eq}-p|
$$

The absolute value is important.

A population may approach equilibrium from below:

$$
p < p_{eq}
$$

or from above:

$$
p>p_{eq}.
$$

In both cases we care about the magnitude of the remaining distance.

This motivates a new odds ratio:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{|p_{eq}-p|}
$$

Interpretation:

- Numerator = where the population currently is.
- Denominator = remaining distance to equilibrium.

Thus:

$$
O_E=\frac{\text{current position}}{\text{remaining distance}}.
$$

---

# 5. Why This Works

Consider several allele frequencies:

| p | Equilibrium Odds (OE) |
|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0.33 |
| 0.40 | 1.00 |
| 0.60 | 3.00 |

The values above are calculated as:

$$
O_E=\frac{0.20}{|0.80-0.20|}=0.33
$$

$$
O_E=\frac{0.40}{|0.80-0.40|}=1.00
$$

$$
O_E=\frac{0.60}{|0.80-0.60|}=3.00
$$

Notice what happens as:

$$
p\rightarrow p_{eq}
$$

The distance:

$$
|p_{eq}-p|
$$

approaches zero.

Therefore:

$$
O_E\rightarrow\infty
$$

This is exactly what happened in the fixation model.

The equilibrium odds become larger and larger as the population approaches equilibrium.

---

# 6. Example Simulation

Suppose we use the exact true-overdominance recursion:

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{p_t(1-p_ts_{ii})}{1-p_t^2s_{ii}-q_t^2s_{jj}}
$$

with

$$
s_{ii}=0.2
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=0.8
$$

Starting from

$$
p_0=0.2,
$$

the first few generations are:

| Generation | p |
|------------|---------|
| 1 | 0.2842 |
| 2 | 0.3943 |
| 3 | 0.4840 |

Recall:

$$
p_{eq}=0.8.
$$

Notice that the population is steadily moving toward equilibrium.

Our goal is to use these observations to estimate how rapidly that approach occurs.

---

# 7. Logarithms Straighten the Curve

Using:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{|p_{eq}-p|}
$$

we obtain:

| Generation | OE |
|------------|---------|
| 1 | 0.551 |
| 2 | 0.972 |
| 3 | 1.532 |

The values above are calculated as:

$$
O_{E,1}=\frac{0.2842}{|0.8000-0.2842|}=0.551
$$

$$
O_{E,2}=\frac{0.3943}{|0.8000-0.3943|}=0.972
$$

$$
O_{E,3}=\frac{0.4840}{|0.8000-0.4840|}=1.532
$$

Now take logarithms:

| Generation | OE | ln(OE) |
|------------|---------|---------|
| 1 | 0.551 | -0.596 |
| 2 | 0.972 | -0.028 |
| 3 | 1.532 | 0.426 |

Notice that the transformed values fall approximately along a straight line.

Once a curved process becomes approximately linear, we can use algebra to estimate the rate of approach to equilibrium.

---

# 8. Estimating the Rate of Approach

Recall the equation of a straight line:

$$
y=a+bx
$$

where:

- $a$ = intercept
- $b$ = slope
- $x$ = independent variable
- $y$ = response variable

For our evolutionary system:

$$
y=\ln(O_E)
$$

and

$$
x=t.
$$

Thus we are asking:

> How rapidly does the logarithm of the equilibrium odds change through time?

The answer is given by the slope.

Using generations 1 and 3:

$$
k=\frac{\ln(O_{E,3})-\ln(O_{E,1})}{3-1}
$$

Substituting:

$$
k=\frac{0.426-(-0.596)}{2}
$$

$$
k=\frac{1.022}{2}
$$

$$
k=0.511
$$

This is our estimate of the average rate at which the population approaches equilibrium.

---

# 9. What Does k Mean?

The parameter

$$
k=0.511
$$

describes how rapidly the population is approaching equilibrium.

Biologically:

- Large $k$ → equilibrium is reached rapidly.
- Small $k$ → equilibrium is reached slowly.

The most important point is:

> The equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends, whereas $k$ tells us how quickly it gets there.

These are different pieces of biological information.

---

# 10. Building the Time-to-Equilibrium Equation

We now write the equation of the line:

$$
\ln(O_E)=a+kt
$$

Using generation 1:

$$
a=\ln(O_{E,1})-k(1)
$$

Substituting:

$$
a=-0.596-0.511
$$

$$
a=-1.107
$$

Thus:

$$
\ln(O_E)=-1.107+0.511t
$$

This equation allows us to project the evolutionary trajectory forward in time.

---

# 11. A Critical Question: Where Does k Come From?

At this point you should ask:

> If I know the selection coefficients and the starting allele frequency, can I calculate k directly?

Usually, no.

The equilibrium frequency:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

comes directly from the balancing-selection model.

However, $k$ describes how rapidly the trajectory moves toward that equilibrium.

Thus:

- Equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends.
- $k$ tells us how quickly evolution gets there.

These quantities are related, but they are not the same thing.

---

# 12. How Do We Estimate k?

There are several ways to estimate $k$ using algebraic methods. More elegant approaches using calculus exist, but they are beyond the scope of this course.

## Method 1: Two Time Points

Calculate equilibrium odds at two generations and compute:

$$
k=\frac{\ln(O_{E,2})-\ln(O_{E,1})}{t_2-t_1}
$$

This is simply the slope formula from algebra.

This is the method used in this lecture.

---

## Method 2: Many Time Points

A better approach is to calculate:

$$
\ln(O_E)
$$

for many generations and fit the line:

$$
\ln(O_E)=a+kt
$$

using linear regression.

Advantages:

- Uses all available information.
- Less sensitive to noise.
- Produces a more stable estimate.

---

## Method 3: Empirical Data

Suppose field biologists measure allele frequencies through time.

If the equilibrium frequency is known:

1. Calculate equilibrium odds.
2. Take logarithms.
3. Estimate the slope.

In this way, $k$ becomes a measurable property of evolution in nature.

---

## Important Caveat

Unlike the selection coefficient $s$, the parameter $k$ is not a fundamental parameter of the balancing-selection model.

Instead, $k$ is an empirical approximation describing the observed trajectory.

Consequently, different portions of the trajectory may produce slightly different estimates of $k$.

For this reason, regression using many points is generally preferred over estimates based on only two points.

---

# 13. Defining "Close Enough"

A subtle problem now appears.

True equilibrium means:

$$
p=0.800000000\ldots
$$

Exactly reaching equilibrium would require:

$$
|p_{eq}-p|=0
$$

which would produce:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{|p_{eq}-p|}=\frac{p}{0}
$$

and therefore:

$$
O_E=\infty
$$

No finite number of generations can produce an infinite odds ratio.

Consequently:

> Equilibrium is never reached exactly.

Instead, we must define what counts as "close enough."

---

# 14. A Practical Definition

Suppose we define equilibrium as being within

$$
0.001
$$

of the true equilibrium.

This corresponds to:

$$
p=0.799
$$

The distance to equilibrium is:

$$
|0.800-0.799|=0.001
$$

The equilibrium odds become:

$$
O_E=\frac{0.799}{0.001}=799
$$

Taking logarithms:

$$
\ln(799)=6.68
$$

This gives us a target value.

---

# 15. Solving for Time

Recall:

$$
\ln(O_E)=-1.107+0.511t
$$

Substituting:

$$
6.68=-1.107+0.511t
$$

Rearrange:

$$
7.787=0.511t
$$

Therefore:

$$
t=15.2
$$

Thus:

$$
t\approx15\text{ generations}
$$

Under our approximation, the population requires roughly fifteen generations to come within 0.001 of the equilibrium frequency.

---

# 16. Biological Interpretation

At generation 1:

$$
p=0.2842
$$

The population is still far from equilibrium.

Consequently, selection acts strongly.

As the population approaches:

$$
p=0.8
$$

the selective imbalance becomes smaller.

Eventually:

$$
qs_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

and therefore:

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

The evolutionary pull toward equilibrium weakens as the population gets closer to its destination.

Consequently:

- Early evolution is rapid.
- Late evolution is slow.
- The final approach to equilibrium takes many generations.

This behavior is common to most stable evolutionary equilibria.

---

# Connection to Time-to-Fixation

Notice the parallels.

| Directional Selection | Balancing Selection |
|---|---|
| Assumes an additive directional-selection model | Approximate equilibrium method |
| Fixation equilibrium | Internal equilibrium |
| Odds ratio | Equilibrium odds ratio |
| fixation | internal equilibrium |
| time-to-fixation | time-to-equilibrium |

For directional selection:

$$
p_{eq}=1
$$

and the odds ratio is:

$$
\frac{p}{1-p}
$$

For balancing selection:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

and the equilibrium odds ratio is:

$$
\frac{p}{|p_{eq}-p|}
$$

In both approaches we:

1. Define an equilibrium.
2. Measure the remaining distance to equilibrium.
3. Construct an odds ratio.
4. Take logarithms to linearize the trajectory.
5. Estimate the time required to become sufficiently close to equilibrium.

Viewed this way, fixation is simply a special case of equilibrium where:

$$
p_{eq}=1
$$

and therefore:

$$
|p_{eq}-p|=|1-p|=1-p.
$$

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

1. True overdominance evolves toward an internal equilibrium rather than fixation.

2. The equilibrium frequency is:

$$
p_{eq}=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

3. The equilibrium odds ratio is:

$$
O_E=\frac{p}{|p_{eq}-p|}
$$

4. As equilibrium is approached:

$$
O_E\rightarrow\infty
$$

5. Logarithms transform the curved approach to equilibrium into an approximately linear relationship.

6. The parameter $k$ measures the rate at which equilibrium is approached.

7. The equilibrium frequency tells us where evolution ends; $k$ tells us how quickly it gets there.

8. $k$ is an empirical approximation usually estimated from simulations, regression, or empirical data.

9. Equilibrium is never reached exactly in a deterministic model.

10. Time to equilibrium therefore means time to become sufficiently close to equilibrium.

11. The same odds-ratio logic used for time-to-fixation can be generalized to estimate time-to-equilibrium.

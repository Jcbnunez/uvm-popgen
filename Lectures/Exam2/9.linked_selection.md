# 1. Evolution at Multiple Loci

Most population-genetic models developed so far have focused on a single locus.

For a single locus, allele-frequency change can usually be described entirely by tracking:

$$
p=f(\alpha_i)
$$

and

$$
q=f(\alpha_j).
$$

However, genomes consist of many loci.

Suppose we now follow two loci simultaneously:

- Locus $\alpha$ with alleles $\alpha_i$ and $\alpha_j$
- Locus $\beta$ with alleles $\beta_i$ and $\beta_j$

The central question becomes:

> Does evolution at locus $\alpha$ influence evolution at locus $\beta$?

The answer depends on whether alleles at the two loci are inherited independently or whether they are statistically associated.

---

# 2. Physical Linkage Versus Linkage Disequilibrium

Before introducing the mathematics, it is important to distinguish two concepts that are often confused.

## Physical Linkage

Physical linkage refers to the locations of loci on chromosomes.

It is quantified by the recombination rate:

$$
r=P(\text{recombination during meiosis})
$$

with

$$
0 \le r \le 0.5.
$$

Interpretation:

- Small $r$ → loci are physically close together.
- Large $r$ → loci are far apart.
- $r=0.5$ → loci assort independently.

---

## Linkage Disequilibrium

Linkage disequilibrium (LD) refers to a statistical association between alleles at different loci.

LD is quantified by statistics such as:

$$
D
$$

or

$$
r^2.
$$

Importantly:

> Physical linkage and linkage disequilibrium are not the same thing.

Two loci may:

- Be tightly linked but have no LD ($D=0$).
- Exhibit LD even if they are not physically close, although recombination rapidly removes such associations.

---

# 3. Haplotypes

For two biallelic loci there are four possible chromosome types.

These chromosome types are called haplotypes.

A haplotype is a multilocus genotype carried on a single chromosome.

The four possible haplotypes are:

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $\alpha_i\beta_i$ | $y_1$ |
| $\alpha_i\beta_j$ | $y_2$ |
| $\alpha_j\beta_i$ | $y_3$ |
| $\alpha_j\beta_j$ | $y_4$ |

Because every chromosome must belong to one of these classes:

$$
y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4=1.
$$

For multilocus evolution, haplotypes become the fundamental quantities we track.

---

# 4. Independent Assortment

Suppose alleles at loci $\alpha$ and $\beta$ are inherited independently.

Then the probability of observing a haplotype equals the product of the corresponding allele frequencies.

For example,

$$
f(\alpha_i\beta_j)=f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_j).
$$

Likewise,

$$
f(\alpha_i\beta_i)=f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_i).
$$

Example:

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $\alpha_i\beta_i$ | 0.25 |
| $\alpha_i\beta_j$ | 0.25 |
| $\alpha_j\beta_i$ | 0.25 |
| $\alpha_j\beta_j$ | 0.25 |

All four combinations occur at frequencies expected from independent assortment.

This condition is called **linkage equilibrium**.

---

# 5. Linkage Equilibrium

Formally, linkage equilibrium occurs when

$$
f(\alpha_i\beta_i)=f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_i).
$$

Equivalently,

$$
f(\beta_i|\alpha_i)=f(\beta_i|\alpha_j).
$$

The frequency of allele $\beta_i$ is the same regardless of which allele occurs at locus $\alpha$.

Knowledge of one locus provides no information about the other locus.

Therefore:

> The two loci evolve independently.

---

# 6. Linkage Disequilibrium

Suppose instead that certain haplotypes occur more frequently than expected.

For example:

| Haplotype | Frequency |
|------------|------------|
| $\alpha_i\beta_i$ | 0.50 |
| $\alpha_i\beta_j$ | 0 |
| $\alpha_j\beta_i$ | 0 |
| $\alpha_j\beta_j$ | 0.50 |

Now only parental combinations occur.

In this case,

$$
f(\alpha_i\beta_i)\neq f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_i).
$$

Likewise,

$$
f(\beta_i|\alpha_i)\neq f(\beta_i|\alpha_j).
$$

The allele present at one locus provides information about the allele present at the second locus.

This condition is called **linkage disequilibrium (LD)**.

---

# 7. Recovering Allele Frequencies from Haplotype Frequencies

Allele frequencies can be expressed as sums of haplotype frequencies.

For locus $\alpha$:

$$
p_\alpha=f(\alpha_i)=y_1+y_2
$$

and

$$
q_\alpha=f(\alpha_j)=y_3+y_4.
$$

Therefore,

$$
p_\alpha+q_\alpha=1.
$$

For locus $\beta$:

$$
p_\beta=f(\beta_i)=y_1+y_3
$$

and

$$
q_\beta=f(\beta_j)=y_2+y_4.
$$

Thus,

$$
p_\beta+q_\beta=1.
$$

---

# 8. Genotype Frequencies Versus Haplotype Frequencies

These quantities describe different biological entities.

## Genotype Frequencies

Single-locus diploid genotypes:

$$
x_{ii},x_{ij},x_{jj}
$$

describe individuals.

---

## Haplotype Frequencies

Multilocus chromosome types:

$$
y_1,y_2,y_3,y_4
$$

describe chromosomes.

When studying linkage, the primary state variables are haplotype frequencies rather than genotype frequencies.

---

# 9. Recombination Generates New Haplotypes

Let

$$
r=\text{recombination rate}.
$$

Interpretation:

$$
r=P(\text{recombination during meiosis}).
$$

Consider haplotype $\alpha_i\beta_i$ with frequency $y_1$.

We wish to determine its frequency in the next generation.

---

## Case 1: No Recombination

Probability:

$$
1-r.
$$

The chromosome remains intact.

Contribution:

$$
(1-r)y_1.
$$

---

## Case 2: Recombination Occurs

Probability:

$$
r.
$$

Recombination reshuffles alleles between loci.

The probability that a recombinant chromosome receives allele $\alpha_i$ equals

$$
p_\alpha.
$$

The probability that it receives allele $\beta_i$ equals

$$
p_\beta.
$$

Assuming random association among recombinant gametes:

$$
P(\alpha_i\beta_i)=p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Contribution:

$$
rp_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

---

## Total Frequency Next Generation

Adding the two contributions:

$$
y_1'=(1-r)y_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

This is the basic recombination recursion.

---

# 10. Evolutionary Rate Equation

Define evolutionary change:

$$
\Delta y_1=y_1'-y_1.
$$

Substitute the recursion:

$$
\Delta y_1=[(1-r)y_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta]-y_1.
$$

Expand:

$$
\Delta y_1=y_1-ry_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta-y_1.
$$

Cancel terms:

$$
\Delta y_1=-ry_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Factor out $r$:

$$
\Delta y_1=-r(y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta).
$$

This result motivates the definition of linkage disequilibrium.

---

# 11. Defining the Disequilibrium Coefficient D

Define

$$
D=y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Substituting into the previous equation gives

$$
\Delta y_1=-rD.
$$

---

## Interpretation of D

The quantity $D$ compares:

Observed haplotype frequency:

$$
y_1=f(\alpha_i\beta_i)
$$

with

Expected haplotype frequency under independence:

$$
p_\alpha p_\beta=f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_i).
$$

Thus,

$$
D=(\text{observed})-(\text{expected}).
$$

---

### If D = 0

$$
y_1=p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

The loci are in linkage equilibrium.

---

### If D > 0

The haplotype occurs more often than expected.

There is an excess association between $\alpha_i$ and $\beta_i$.

---

### If D < 0

The haplotype occurs less often than expected.

There is a deficit of association.

---

# 12. Why Recombination Reduces LD

Recall:

$$
\Delta y_1=-rD.
$$

Notice that the sign of the change is always opposite to the sign of $D$.

If $D>0$, recombination decreases the excess haplotype.

If $D<0$, recombination increases the deficient haplotype.

Therefore recombination always acts to reduce statistical associations.

This leads to one of the most important conclusions in population genetics:

> Recombination destroys associations among loci.

---

# 13. Decay of Linkage Disequilibrium

We can derive the recursion for LD itself.

Start with:

$$
D'=y_1'-p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Substitute the recombination recursion:

$$
D'=(1-r)y_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta-p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Factor terms:

$$
D'=(1-r)(y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta).
$$

Since

$$
D=y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta
$$

we obtain:

$$
D'=(1-r)D.
$$

---

## Through-Time Decay

Applying the recursion repeatedly:

$$
D_t=(1-r)^tD_0.
$$

Biological implications:

- Large $r$ → rapid LD decay.
- Small $r$ → slow LD decay.
- Tightly linked loci maintain LD for many generations.
- Unlinked loci lose LD rapidly.

---

# 14. Alternative Formula for D

Start with

$$
D=y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

Substitute

$$
p_\alpha=y_1+y_2
$$

and

$$
p_\beta=y_1+y_3.
$$

Then

$$
D=y_1-(y_1+y_2)(y_1+y_3).
$$

Expanding and simplifying using

$$
y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4=1
$$

gives

$$
D=y_1y_4-y_2y_3.
$$

Thus

$$
D=f(\alpha_i\beta_i)f(\alpha_j\beta_j)-f(\alpha_i\beta_j)f(\alpha_j\beta_i).
$$

---

## Biological Interpretation

Parental (non-recombinant) haplotypes:

$$
\alpha_i\beta_i,\alpha_j\beta_j
$$

Recombinant haplotypes:

$$
\alpha_i\beta_j,\alpha_j\beta_i.
$$

Therefore:

$$
D=(\text{parental product})-(\text{recombinant product}).
$$

Large positive values indicate an excess of parental haplotypes.

---

# 15. Expressing Haplotype Frequencies Using D

The four haplotypes can be written as:

$$
y_1=p_\alpha p_\beta+D
$$

$$
y_2=p_\alpha q_\beta-D
$$

$$
y_3=q_\alpha p_\beta-D
$$

$$
y_4=q_\alpha q_\beta+D.
$$

Adding them together:

$$
y_1+y_2+y_3+y_4=1.
$$

Thus the parameterization is internally consistent.

---

# 16. Measuring the Strength of LD

Although $D$ is the fundamental measure of LD, its magnitude depends on allele frequencies.

Consequently, population geneticists often use standardized measures.

---

## The Correlation Statistic r²

One common measure is

$$
r^2=\frac{D^2}{p_\alpha q_\alpha p_\beta q_\beta}.
$$

Properties:

$$
0\le r^2\le 1.
$$

Interpretation:

- $r^2=0$ → no association.
- $r^2=1$ → perfect association.
- Intermediate values → partial association.

---

## Important Warning

Students frequently confuse:

$$
r
$$

and

$$
r^2.
$$

These are completely different quantities.

| Quantity | Meaning |
|-----------|-----------|
| $r$ | recombination rate |
| $r^2$ | LD correlation statistic |

The recombination rate measures a biological process.

The LD statistic measures a statistical association.

---

# 17. Linked Selection

Selection acting on one locus can indirectly affect nearby neutral loci.

Why?

Because recombination is imperfect.

A neutral allele can be physically linked to an adaptive allele.

If the adaptive allele increases in frequency, nearby neutral alleles may increase along with it.

This phenomenon is called **genetic hitchhiking**.

---

# 18. Fitness Model for Linked Selection

Assume:

- Locus $\alpha$ affects fitness.
- Locus $\beta$ is neutral.
- Selection coefficient = $s$.
- Dominance coefficient = $h$.

Only locus $\alpha$ contributes directly to fitness.

Any evolutionary change at locus $\beta$ must therefore occur indirectly through linkage.

---

# 19. Selection on the Adaptive Locus

For directional selection at a single locus,

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pqs[ph+q(1-h)]}{1-2pqhs-q^2s}.
$$

Here:

- $p$ = frequency of advantageous allele.
- $q=1-p$.
- $s$ = selection coefficient.
- $h$ = dominance coefficient.

---

For locus $\alpha$:

$$
p_\alpha=y_1+y_2.
$$

Therefore

$$
\Delta p_\alpha=\Delta y_1+\Delta y_2.
$$

Substituting into the single-locus equation gives

$$
\Delta y_1+\Delta y_2=\frac{p_\alpha q_\alpha s[p_\alpha h+q_\alpha(1-h)]}{1-2p_\alpha q_\alpha hs-q_\alpha^2s}.
$$

---

## Biological Interpretation

Selection acts directly on locus $\alpha$.

Consequently, allele-frequency change depends on:

- selection strength ($s$),
- dominance ($h$),
- allele frequencies ($p_\alpha,q_\alpha$).

Notice that no LD term appears.

The adaptive locus changes because it is itself the target of selection.

---

# 20. Evolution of the Neutral Hitchhiking Allele

Now consider locus $\beta$.

By assumption, locus $\beta$ has no direct effect on fitness.

Why should it evolve?

Define:

$$
p_\beta=y_1+y_3.
$$

Since

$$
\Delta p_\beta=\Delta y_1+\Delta y_3
$$

the change at the neutral locus is

$$
\Delta y_1+\Delta y_3=\frac{Ds[p_\alpha h+q_\alpha(1-h)]}{1-2p_\alpha q_\alpha hs-q_\alpha^2s}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\Delta p_\beta=\frac{Ds[p_\alpha h+q_\alpha(1-h)]}{1-2p_\alpha q_\alpha hs-q_\alpha^2s}.
$$

---

## Why Does D Appear?

Selection acts only on locus $\alpha$.

Therefore the only way locus $\beta$ can respond is if alleles at $\beta$ are statistically associated with alleles at $\alpha$.

That association is measured by:

$$
D.
$$

This is the central insight of linked selection.

---

## Comparing the Two Equations

Adaptive locus:

$$
\Delta p_\alpha\propto p_\alpha q_\alpha.
$$

Neutral locus:

$$
\Delta p_\beta\propto D.
$$

The adaptive locus evolves because selection acts directly upon it.

The neutral locus evolves only because LD couples it to the adaptive locus.

---

# 21. Three Important Cases

## Case 1: No Selection

If

$$
s=0
$$

then

$$
\Delta p_\beta=0.
$$

No hitchhiking occurs.

---

## Case 2: Selection But No LD

If

$$
D=0
$$

then

$$
\Delta p_\beta=0.
$$

Selection acts on $\alpha$ but cannot influence $\beta$.

---

## Case 3: Selection and LD

If

$$
D\neq0
$$

and

$$
s\neq0
$$

then

$$
\Delta p_\beta\neq0.
$$

A neutral allele changes frequency despite having no fitness effect.

This is genetic hitchhiking.

---

# 22. Intuitive Example of Hitchhiking

Suppose a beneficial mutation arises on a chromosome carrying allele $\beta_i$.

Initially, $\beta_i$ has no fitness effect.

However, whenever natural selection increases the beneficial allele at locus $\alpha$, the linked neutral allele $\beta_i$ is also copied into more offspring.

As a result:

- the selected allele rises because of fitness,
- the neutral allele rises because of association.

The stronger the LD (larger $|D|$), the stronger the hitchhiking effect.

---

# 23. Biological Consequences of Linked Selection

## Background Selection

Purifying selection continually removes deleterious mutations.

Linked neutral variants are removed along with them.

Result:

- reduced neutral diversity near selected sites.

---

## Hard Selective Sweep

A new beneficial mutation arises and rapidly fixes.

Nearby neutral variation hitchhikes to high frequency.

Results:

- reduced genetic diversity,
- long haplotypes,
- elevated local LD.

---

## Soft Selective Sweep

Adaptation occurs from multiple beneficial haplotypes.

Results:

- weaker diversity reduction,
- more heterogeneous haplotype structure,
- weaker LD signatures.

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

1. Linkage equilibrium occurs when

$$
f(\alpha_i\beta_j)=f(\alpha_i)f(\beta_j).
$$

2. Linkage disequilibrium occurs when haplotypes deviate from independent expectations.

3. Physical linkage and LD are different concepts:
   - linkage is measured by $r$,
   - LD is measured by $D$ or $r^2$.

4. The disequilibrium coefficient is

$$
D=y_1-p_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

or equivalently

$$
D=y_1y_4-y_2y_3.
$$

5. Recombination changes haplotype frequencies according to

$$
y_1'=(1-r)y_1+rp_\alpha p_\beta.
$$

6. Recombination erodes LD:

$$
\Delta y_1=-rD.
$$

7. LD decays geometrically through time:

$$
D'=(1-r)D
$$

and

$$
D_t=(1-r)^tD_0.
$$

8. The statistic

$$
r^2=\frac{D^2}{p_\alpha q_\alpha p_\beta q_\beta}
$$

is not the square of the recombination rate.

9. Selection acting on one locus can influence a neutral locus whenever

$$
D\neq0.
$$

10. Genetic hitchhiking occurs because LD couples neutral and selected loci.

11. Selection tends to create associations among loci, whereas recombination continuously breaks those associations apart.

12. Much of modern population genomics is built on understanding this tension between selection and recombination.

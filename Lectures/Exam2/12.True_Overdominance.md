# 1. Beyond Directional Selection

So far we have studied systems in which one allele consistently has higher fitness than another.

Under directional selection:

- one allele is favored,
- one allele is disfavored,
- the favored allele tends toward fixation.

Consequently, directional selection generally reduces genetic variation through time.

A natural question therefore emerges:

> Can natural selection ever maintain multiple alleles indefinitely?

The answer is yes.

This class of processes is called balancing selection.

---

# 2. What is Balancing Selection?

Balancing selection refers to situations in which the evolutionary process maintains multiple alleles within a population rather than driving one allele to fixation. 

In this lecture we focus on the simplest form:

## True Overdominance

True overdominance occurs when the heterozygote has higher fitness than either homozygote. 

Mathematically:

$$
w_{ij} > w_{ii}
$$

and

$$
w_{ij} > w_{jj}.
$$

The heterozygote is the fittest genotype.

---

# 3. General Fitness Scheme

Let:

- allele $\alpha_i$ occur with frequency $p$
- allele $\alpha_j$ occur with frequency $q$

where

$$
p+q=1.
$$

Assume genotype fitnesses:

$$
w_{ii}=1-s_{ii}
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s_{jj}.
$$

This parameterization places the heterozygote at maximum fitness.

The quantities

$$
s_{ii}
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}
$$

measure the fitness disadvantage of each homozygote.

This is the defining assumption of true overdominance. 

---

# 4. The Fundamental Axiom of Overdominance

In directional selection we typically assumed:

$$
s_{ii}=0
$$

meaning one homozygote has maximum fitness.

For true overdominance the situation is fundamentally different.

The heterozygote is best:

$$
s_{ij}=0.
$$

Thus:

$$
w_{ij}=1.
$$

This change in assumption completely alters the evolutionary behavior of the system.

---

# 5. Genotype Frequencies After Selection

Before selection:

$$
x_{ii}=p^2
$$

$$
x_{ij}=2pq
$$

$$
x_{jj}=q^2.
$$

After viability selection (**recall that $w_{ij}=1.0$**):

$$
x'_{ii}=\frac{p^2w_{ii}}{\bar w}=\frac{p^2(1-s_{ii})}{\bar w}
$$

$$
x'_{ij}=\frac{2pqw_{ij}}{\bar w}=\frac{2pq}{\bar w}
$$

$$
x'_{jj}=\frac{q^2w_{jj}}{\bar w}=\frac{q^2(1-s_{jj})}{\bar w}.
$$

Here, as usual,

$$
\bar w
$$

is mean fitness.

---

# 6. Deriving Mean Fitness

Mean fitness equals the weighted average fitness of all genotypes.

Start from:

$$
\bar w=p^2(1-s_{ii})
+
2pq(1-s_{ij})
+
q^2(1-s_{jj}).
$$

Because true overdominance assumes

$$
s_{ij}=0
$$

we obtain

$$
\bar w=
p^2(1-s_{ii})+
2pq+
q^2(1-s_{jj}).
$$

Expand:

$$
\bar w=
p^2-p^2s_{ii}+
2pq+
q^2-q^2s_{jj}.
$$

Group terms:

$$
\bar w=
(p^2+2pq+q^2)-
p^2s_{ii}-
q^2s_{jj}.
$$

Since

$$
p^2+2pq+q^2=(p+q)^2=1
$$

we obtain

$$
\bar w=
1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}.
$$

This is the mean fitness equation for true overdominance.

---

# 7. Deriving the Predictive Equation

The frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ after selection equals the contribution of all genotypes carrying $\alpha_i$.

Thus

$$
p_{t+1}=
x'_{ii}+\frac12 x'_{ij}.
$$

Substitute the post-selection genotype frequencies:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2(1-s_{ii})}{\bar w}+
\frac12
\left(
\frac{2pq}{\bar w}
\right).
$$

Simplify:

$$
p_{t+1}=\frac{p^2(1-s_{ii})+pq}{\bar w}.
$$

Expand:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2-p^2s_{ii}+pq}{\bar w}.
$$

Factor:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p(p-ps_{ii}+q)}{\bar w}.
$$

Since

$$
p+q=1
$$

we obtain

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p(1-ps_{ii})}
{\bar w}.
$$

Replacing $\bar w$ gives

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p(1-ps_{ii})}
{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

This predicts allele frequency one generation into the future. 

---

# 8. Deriving the Evolutionary Rate Equation

Define evolutionary change:

$$
\Delta p=
p_{t+1}-p.
$$

Substitute the predictive equation:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{p(1-ps_{ii})}
{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}
-p.
$$

Place everything over a common denominator:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
p(1-ps_{ii})-p(1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj})}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

Expand:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
p-p^2s_{ii}-p+p^3s_{ii}+pq^2s_{jj}}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

The $p$ terms cancel:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
-p^2s_{ii}
+p^3s_{ii}
+pq^2s_{jj}}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

Factor:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
p^2s_{ii}(p-1)+
pq^2s_{jj}}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

Since

$$
p-1=-q
$$

we obtain

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{-p^2qs_{ii}+pq^2s_{jj}}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

Factor $pq$:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

This is the evolutionary rate equation for true overdominance. 

---

# 9. Finding the Equilibrium

At equilibrium:

$$
\Delta p=0.
$$

Substitute the rate equation:

$$
0=\frac{
pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})
}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

The denominator is positive whenever mean fitness is positive.

Therefore equilibrium requires

$$
pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})=0.
$$

There are three possible solutions:

$$
p=0
$$

$$
q=0
$$

or

$$
qs_{jj}-ps_{ii}=0.
$$

The first two correspond to fixation.

The third solution gives the internal balancing equilibrium.

---

# 10. Solving for the Internal Equilibrium

Start with

$$
qs_{jj}=ps_{ii}.
$$

Substitute

$$
q=1-p.
$$

Then

$$
(1-p)s_{jj}=ps_{ii}.
$$

Expand:

$$
s_{jj}-ps_{jj}=ps_{ii}.
$$

Move terms:

$$
s_{jj}=ps_{ii}+ps_{jj}.
$$

Factor:

$$
s_{jj}=p(s_{ii}+s_{jj}).
$$

Therefore

$$
p_{eq}=
\frac{s_{jj}}
{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
q_{eq}=
\frac{s_{ii}}
{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

These are among the most important equations in population genetics. 

---

# 11. Why Is This Equilibrium Stable?

Consider

$$
p < p_{eq}.
$$

Then

$$
qs_{jj}>ps_{ii}
$$

which implies

$$
\Delta p>0.
$$

Selection increases $p$.

---

Now suppose

$$
p>p_{eq}.
$$

Then

$$
qs_{jj} < ps_{ii}
$$

which implies

$$
\Delta p<0.
$$

Selection decreases $p$.

---

Therefore:

$$
p < p_{eq}
\Rightarrow
\Delta p>0
$$

and

$$
p>p_{eq}
\Rightarrow
\Delta p<0.
$$

Selection always pushes the population back toward equilibrium.

This is a stable equilibrium. 


# 12. The Classic Example: Sickle Cell and Malaria

One of the most famous examples of true overdominance involves the human hemoglobin locus. The slides use the malaria-sickle cell system as the motivating biological example.

In regions with intense malaria exposure:

Let

$$
\alpha_i=
\text{normal hemoglobin allele}
$$

and

$$
\alpha_j=
\text{sickle-cell allele}.
$$

Observed fitnesses are approximately:

$$
w_{ii}=0.8
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1.0
$$

$$
w_{jj}=0.2.
$$

Thus

$$
s_{ii}=0.2
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=0.8.
$$

The heterozygote has the highest fitness because it receives malaria resistance while largely avoiding severe sickle-cell disease. 

---

# 13. Calculating the Equilibrium

Substitute:

$$
p_{eq}=
\frac{0.8}
{0.8+0.2}.
$$

Simplify:

$$
p_{eq}=
0.8.
$$

Therefore:

$$
q_{eq}=
0.2.
$$

The population is predicted to maintain both alleles indefinitely:

- 80% normal Hb allele
- 20% sickle-cell allele

provided the fitness values remain constant. 

---

# 14. Why Overdominance Maintains Variation

Under directional selection:

$$
p=0
$$

or

$$
p=1
$$

are the typical endpoints.

Variation is lost.

---

Under true overdominance:

$$
0 < p_{eq}<1.
$$

Both alleles are maintained permanently.

Therefore:

> Selection actively preserves genetic variation rather than removing it.

This is the defining signature of balancing selection.

---

# Key Take-Home Messages

1. True overdominance occurs when

$$
w_{ij}>w_{ii}
$$

and

$$
w_{ij}>w_{jj}.
$$

2. The heterozygote is the fittest genotype.

3. The defining assumption is

$$
s_{ij}=0.
$$

4. Mean fitness under true overdominance is

$$
\bar w=
1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}.
$$

5. The predictive recursion is

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p(1-ps_{ii})}
{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

6. The evolutionary rate equation is

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})}
{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}.
$$

7. The equilibrium frequency is

$$
p_{eq}=
\frac{s_{jj}}
{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

8. The complementary equilibrium is

$$
q_{eq}=
\frac{s_{ii}}
{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}.
$$

9. This equilibrium is stable because selection pushes allele frequencies back toward it.

10. True overdominance is one of the few deterministic evolutionary mechanisms that actively maintains genetic variation in populations.

# Balancing Selection in Spatially Heterogeneous Environments

## Learning Objectives

By the end of this lecture, you should be able to:

1. Distinguish directional selection, true overdominance, and marginal overdominance.
2. Explain how spatial variation in selection can maintain polymorphism.
3. Calculate genotype fitness across heterogeneous habitats.
4. Derive equilibrium allele frequencies under marginal overdominance.
5. Understand the biological assumptions underlying Levene's model.

---

# 1. Review: Directional vs. Balancing Selection

Recall that selection changes allele frequencies according to differences in genotype fitness.

For a locus $\alpha$ with two alleles:

- $\alpha_i$
- $\alpha_j$

Let

- $p =$ frequency of $\alpha_i$
- $q =$ frequency of $\alpha_j$
- $p+q=1$

Under directional selection, one allele is consistently favored in all environments.

Under balancing selection, multiple alleles are maintained and neither fixes permanently.

Conceptually:

Directional selection:

``` text
Allele frequency
1.0 |           *
    |         *
    |       *
    |     *
    |   *
0.0 +-------------------
      Time
```

Balancing selection:

``` text
Allele frequency
1.0 |
    |
0.5 |-----------*------
    |
0.0 +-------------------
      Time
```

The equilibrium is stable because selection pushes allele frequencies back toward an intermediate value.

---

# 2. Types of Balancing Selection

Several mechanisms can maintain genetic variation.

## True Overdominance

Genotype fitnesses:

$$
w_{ii} < w_{ij} > w_{jj}
$$

The heterozygote is intrinsically superior in every environment.

Example:

$$
w_{ii}=0.8, w_{ij}=1.0, w_{jj}=0.7
$$

The heterozygote advantage directly creates balancing selection.

---

## Marginal Overdominance

Marginal overdominance is different.

No habitat individually exhibits heterozygote superiority.

Instead:

- Different habitats favor different homozygotes.
- Averaging fitness across habitats produces an apparent heterozygote advantage.

Thus:

$$
w_{ij}>w_{ii},w_{jj}
$$

only after **averaging** across environments.

The heterozygote is not intrinsically superior.

The heterozygote is superior only when fitness is evaluated across the entire heterogeneous habitat.

This phenomenon was formalized by Levene (1953).

---

# 3. Biological Motivation

Consider a barnacle population occupying an intertidal habitat.

Some microhabitats experience:

- severe thermal stress
- frequent exposure
- desiccation

Other microhabitats experience:

- lower thermal stress
- more constant moisture

Suppose:

- allele $\alpha_i$ performs best in high-stress habitats
- allele $\alpha_j$ performs best in low-stress habitats

Selection therefore differs among habitat patches.

The question becomes:

How do we predict allele frequencies across the entire landscape?

---

# 4. Levene Model Assumptions

The classical Levene model assumes:

1. Random mating across the species.
2. A heterogeneous environment composed of several habitat types.
3. Selection occurs within each habitat.
4. Habitat types contribute offspring proportional to habitat size.
5. Generations are discrete.
6. Genotype fitness differs among habitats.

Under these assumptions, spatial environmental variation can maintain polymorphism.

---

# 5. Partitioning the Habitat

Suppose the total niche contains two habitat classes.

High-stress habitat:

$$
\phi_H=0.5
$$

Low-stress habitat:

$$
\phi_L=0.5
$$

Because these are proportions of the total habitat:

$$
\phi_H+\phi_L=1
$$

where:

- $\phi_H$ = fraction of high-stress habitat
- $\phi_L$ = fraction of low-stress habitat

---

# 6. Habitat-Specific Fitnesses

Assume genotype fitnesses are:

| Habitat | $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ |
|----------|----------|----------|----------|
| High stress | 1.0 | 0.9 | 0.5 |
| Low stress | 0.5 | 0.9 | 1.0 |

Interpretation:

In the high-stress habitat:

$$
w_{ii}>w_{ij}>w_{jj}
$$

Allele $\alpha_i$ is favored.

In the low-stress habitat:

$$
w_{jj}>w_{ij}>w_{ii}
$$

Allele $\alpha_j$ is favored.

Notice:

$$
w_{ij}\neq \max(w)
$$

within either habitat.

Therefore this is NOT true overdominance.

---

# 7. Constructing Global Fitness

To determine selection across the entire niche, we calculate the weighted average genotype fitness.

For genotype $\alpha_i\alpha_i$:

$$
w_{ii}= \phi_H w_{ii|H}+\phi_L w_{ii|L}
$$

Substituting values:

$$
w_{ii}=0.5(1.0)+0.5(0.5)
$$

$$
w_{ii}=0.75
$$

---

For genotype $\alpha_i\alpha_j$:

$$
w_{ij}=0.5(0.9)+0.5(0.9)
$$

$$
w_{ij}=0.90
$$

---

For genotype $\alpha_j\alpha_j$:

$$
w_{jj}=0.5(0.5)+0.5(1.0)
$$

$$
w_{jj}=0.75
$$

Thus:

| Genotype | Global Fitness |
|----------|----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | 0.75 |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | 0.90 |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | 0.75 |

---

# 8. Emergence of Marginal Overdominance

After averaging across habitats:

$$
0.75<0.90>0.75
$$

The heterozygote now has highest fitness.

Importantly:

The heterozygote was never superior within any habitat.

Rather, environmental averaging generated an effective heterozygote advantage.

This is why the mechanism is called "marginal" overdominance.

The superiority emerges only after averaging over habitat states.

---

# 9. Reparameterizing the Fitnesses

The balancing-selection model developed previously assumes:

$$
w_{ij}=1
$$

Therefore we normalize fitnesses by dividing every genotype fitness by the largest value.

The largest fitness is:

$$
0.90
$$

Normalize:

For $\alpha_i\alpha_i$:

$$
w'_{ii}=\frac{0.75}{0.90}
$$

$$
w'_{ii}=0.833
$$

For the heterozygote:

$$
w'_{ij}=\frac{0.90}{0.90}
$$

$$
w'_{ij}=1
$$

For $\alpha_j\alpha_j$:

$$
w'_{jj}=\frac{0.75}{0.90}
$$

$$
w'_{jj}=0.833
$$

Thus:

| Genotype | Relative Fitness |
|----------|----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | 0.833 |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | 1.000 |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | 0.833 |

---

# 10. Expressing Fitness in Terms of Selection Coefficients

Using the overdominance parameterization:

$$
w_{ii}=1-s_{ii}
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s_{jj}
$$

Therefore:

$$
s_{ii}=1-0.833
$$

$$
s_{ii}=0.167
$$

and

$$
s_{jj}=1-0.833
$$

$$
s_{jj}=0.167
$$

---

# 11. Deriving the Equilibrium Frequency

Under balancing selection:

$$
\Delta p=\frac{pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})}{1-p^2s_{ii}-q^2s_{jj}}
$$

At equilibrium:

$$
\Delta p=0
$$

The denominator is positive for biologically reasonable fitnesses.

Therefore equilibrium occurs when the numerator equals zero:

$$
pq(qs_{jj}-ps_{ii})=0
$$

Three solutions are possible:

Boundary equilibria:

$$
p=0
$$

or

$$
q=0
$$

The internal equilibrium requires:

$$
qs_{jj}-ps_{ii}=0
$$

Rearrange:

$$
qs_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

Substitute:

$$
q=1-p
$$

giving

$$
(1-p)s_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

Expand:

$$
s_{jj}-ps_{jj}=ps_{ii}
$$

Collect terms involving $p$:

$$
s_{jj}=p(s_{ii}+s_{jj})
$$

Divide both sides:

$$
p=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

This is the classic overdominance equilibrium.

---

# 12. Calculating the Equilibrium

Substitute:

$$
s_{ii}=0.167
$$

$$
s_{jj}=0.167
$$

Therefore:

$$
\hat p=\frac{0.167}{0.167+0.167}
$$

$$
\hat p=\frac{0.167}{0.334}
$$

$$
\hat p=0.5
$$

Because:

$$
\hat q=1-\hat p
$$

we obtain:

$$
\hat q=0.5
$$

Thus:

$$
\hat p=\hat q=0.5
$$

---

# 13. Why the Equilibrium Equals 0.5

The result follows from perfect symmetry. **WARNING: This is just a special case and individual examples will show different equilibria!**

High-stress habitat favors $\alpha_i$ exactly as strongly as low-stress habitat favors $\alpha_j$.

Likewise:

$$
s_{ii}=s_{jj}
$$

When selection coefficients are identical:

$$
\hat p=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
=\frac{s_{jj}}{2s_{jj}}
=\frac{1}{2}
$$

Neither allele has a net long-term advantage.

Consequently, both persist indefinitely.

---

# 14. Stability of the Equilibrium

Suppose:

$$
p<0.5
$$

Then:

- allele $\alpha_i$ is rare
- allele $\alpha_j$ is common

The term:

$$
qs_{jj}-ps_{ii}
$$

becomes positive.

Therefore:

$$
\Delta p>0
$$

and allele $\alpha_i$ increases.

Conversely, if:

$$
p>0.5
$$

then:

$$
\Delta p<0
$$

and allele $\alpha_i$ decreases.

Thus selection pushes the population back toward:

$$
p=0.5
$$

making the equilibrium stable.

```text
    +--------------------

```

# 15. Key Conceptual Distinction

## True Overdominance

Within every environment:

$$
w_{ij}>w_{ii},w_{jj}
$$

The heterozygote itself is intrinsically superior.

---

## Marginal Overdominance

Within habitats:

Different homozygotes are favored.

After averaging across habitats:

$$
w_{ij}>w_{ii},w_{jj}
$$

The heterozygote appears superior only at the whole-population scale.

---

# Take-Home Messages

1. Spatially heterogeneous environments can maintain polymorphism.
2. Levene's model provides the classic framework for understanding balancing selection across habitat patches.
3. Marginal overdominance arises from averaging fitness across habitats, not from intrinsic heterozygote superiority.
4. Global fitness is calculated as a weighted average:

$$
w=\sum_k \phi_k w_k
$$

5. The resulting fitnesses can be reparameterized into the standard overdominance model.
6. Internal equilibrium occurs when:

$$
\hat p=\frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii}+s_{jj}}
$$

meaning both alleles are permanently maintained by balancing selection.

# Historical Motivation

One of the central questions in population genetics is:

> Why does genetic variation persist?

If one allele is consistently favored, directional selection generally drives that allele toward fixation.

However, natural populations often contain substantial genetic variation despite persistent selection.

Several mechanisms can maintain polymorphism:

- mutation-selection balance
- migration-selection balance
- negative frequency-dependent selection
- overdominance
- spatially varying environments
- temporally varying environments

We ask whether fluctuating environments alone could maintain a stable polymorphism.

His analysis revealed that temporal environmental variation can generate rare-allele advantages, but these effects are often weaker than biologists originally expected.

---

# Biological Scenario

Consider a single locus, $\alpha$.

Two alleles segregate:

$$
\alpha_i
$$

and

$$
\alpha_j
$$

with frequencies

$$
p=f(\alpha_i)
$$

and

$$
q=f(\alpha_j).
$$

Because only two alleles are present,

$$
p+q=1
$$

and therefore

$$
q=1-p.
$$

The environment changes among generations.

For example:

| Generation | Favored Allele |
|---|---|
| Wet year | $\alpha_i$ |
| Dry year | $\alpha_j$ |

Thus the direction and strength of selection vary through time.

---

# Model Assumptions

We assume:

1. Random mating.
2. Infinite population size.
3. No mutation.
4. No migration.
5. No genetic drift.
6. Discrete generations.
7. The entire population experiences the same environment each generation.
8. Dominance remains constant through time.
9. Selection coefficients fluctuate among generations.

The final assumption allows us to isolate the effects of temporal variation in selection.

---

# Fitness Parameterization

Instead of assigning arbitrary genotype fitnesses, we express fitness in terms of:

- selection coefficient $s_t$
- dominance coefficient $h$

Assume genotype fitnesses during generation $t$ are

$$
w_{ii,t}=1+s_t
$$

$$
w_{ij,t}=1+hs_t
$$

$$
w_{jj,t}=1.
$$

where:

- $s_t$ may vary among generations
- $h$ remains constant

Interpretation:

- $s_t > 0$ favors $\alpha_i$
- $s_t < 0$ favors $\alpha_j$

The environment therefore affects evolution entirely through changes in $s_t$.

---

# Why This Parameterization Is Useful

If environmental conditions switch which allele is favored, then the sign of $s_t$ simply changes.

For example:

| Environment | $s_t$ |
|---|---|
| Wet year | +0.20 |
| Dry year | -0.20 |

When

$$
s_t=+0.20
$$

genotype fitnesses are

$$
w_{ii,t}=1.20
$$

$$
w_{ij,t}=1+0.20h
$$

$$
w_{jj,t}=1.
$$

When

$$
s_t=-0.20
$$

the ranking reverses.

This produces temporal fluctuations without changing dominance relationships.

---

# Genotype Frequencies Before Selection

Random mating produces Hardy-Weinberg genotype frequencies:

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_i:p_t^2
$$

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_j:2p_tq_t
$$

$$
\alpha_j\alpha_j:q_t^2.
$$

After selection these become proportional to

$$
p_t^2(1+s_t)
$$

$$
2p_tq_t(1+hs_t)
$$

$$
q_t^2.
$$

To convert these weighted values into frequencies, we must calculate the population mean fitness.

---

# Deriving Mean Fitness

Mean fitness equals genotype frequency multiplied by genotype fitness, summed across all genotypes:

$$
\bar w_t=p_t^2(1+s_t)+2p_tq_t(1+hs_t)+q_t^2.
$$

Expand:

$$
\bar w_t=p_t^2+p_t^2s_t+2p_tq_t+2hp_tq_ts_t+q_t^2.
$$

Using

$$
p_t^2+2p_tq_t+q_t^2=1
$$

gives

$$
\bar w_t=1+s_t(p_t^2+2hp_tq_t).
$$

---

# What Is Marginal Fitness and Why Do We Need It?

Up to this point, selection has been defined at the level of genotypes.

However, the quantity we ultimately wish to predict is allele-frequency change.

This creates an important conceptual problem:

> Selection acts on genotypes, but evolution is usually measured as changes in allele frequencies.

To connect these two levels of organization, we need a quantity that measures the average success of an allele across all genetic backgrounds in which it occurs.

That quantity is called **marginal fitness**.

---

# Deriving Marginal Fitness

Before selection, genotype frequencies are

$$
p^2
$$

$$
2pq
$$

$$
q^2.
$$

After selection, genotype frequencies become proportional to

$$
p^2w_{ii}
$$

$$
2pqw_{ij}
$$

$$
q^2w_{jj}.
$$

To determine the frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ among survivors, we count all surviving copies of that allele.

The genotype $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ contributes two copies of $\alpha_i$.

The genotype $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ contributes one copy of $\alpha_i$.

Therefore

$$
p'=\frac{p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}}{\bar w}.
$$

Factoring out $p$ gives

$$
p'=\frac{p(pw_{ii}+qw_{ij})}{\bar w}.
$$

The quantity

$$
pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}
$$

appears naturally from the algebra.

We therefore define

$$
w_{\alpha_i}=pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
w_{\alpha_j}=pw_{ij}+qw_{jj}.
$$

These are the marginal fitnesses of the two alleles.

Substituting gives

$$
p'=\frac{pw_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}
$$

and

$$
q'=\frac{qw_{\alpha_j}}{\bar w}.
$$

---

# Why Is There No Division by Mean Fitness in Marginal Fitness?

Marginal fitness and mean fitness answer different biological questions.

Marginal fitness asks:

> How successful is a particular allele?

For example,

$$
w_{\alpha_i}=pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}
$$

is already a weighted average because

$$
p+q=1.
$$

Nothing needs to be normalized.

Mean fitness asks:

> How successful is the population as a whole?

The weighted genotype frequencies after selection do not sum to one. They sum to

$$
\bar w.
$$

Therefore division by $\bar w$ is required only when converting weighted genotype contributions into frequencies.

---

# Why Marginal Fitness Determines Evolution

Starting from

$$
p'=\frac{pw_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}
$$

divide both sides by $p$:

$$
\frac{p'}{p}=\frac{w_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}.
$$

If

$$
w_{\alpha_i}>\bar w
$$

then

$$
p'>p.
$$

If

$$
w_{\alpha_i}<\bar w
$$

then

$$
p' < p.
$$

Thus an allele increases whenever its marginal fitness exceeds the population mean fitness.

This result links genotype fitnesses to allele-frequency evolution.

---

# Marginal Fitness:

Substitute

$$
w_{ii,t}=1+s_t
$$

$$
w_{ij,t}=1+hs_t
$$

$$
w_{jj,t}=1.
$$

**For allele $\alpha_i$:**

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=p_t(1+s_t)+q_t(1+hs_t).
$$

Expanding gives

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=1+s_t(p_t+hq_t).
$$

**For allele $\alpha_j$:**

$$
w_{\alpha_j,t}=p_t(1+hs_t)+q_t.
$$

---

# Why Do We Focus on Rare Alleles?

Why do population geneticists focus on rare alleles rather than common alleles when studying balancing selection?

The reason is that the persistence of genetic variation depends on what happens when an allele becomes uncommon. A common allele is not in immediate danger of disappearing because many copies already exist in the population. In contrast, a rare allele is at risk of being lost entirely. Therefore, the critical test is whether an allele can recover after its frequency has fallen to a very low level. Consider a wet-dry environment in which allele $\alpha_i$ performs better in wet years and allele $\alpha_j$ performs better in dry years. Suppose $\alpha_i$ becomes very rare after several dry years. If wet years return and $\alpha_i$ can increase from rarity, then the allele is protected from extinction and polymorphism can be maintained. If $\alpha_i$ continues to decline even when conditions favor it, then it will eventually disappear from the population. Thus, to determine whether a mechanism maintains genetic variation, we do not ask whether common alleles remain common; we ask whether rare alleles can invade and recover. A polymorphism is stable only when both alleles gain an advantage when rare.

---

# Rare Allele Dynamics

Suppose allele $\alpha_i$ is very rare.

Then

$$
p \approx 0
$$

and

$$
q \approx 1.
$$

Substituting into the marginal-fitness equation,

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=1+s_t(p_t+hq_t)
$$

gives

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}\approx1+hs_t.
$$

Thus a rare allele experiences approximately the fitness of a heterozygote.

Its growth factors from generation to generation are therefore

$$
1+hs_1
$$

$$
1+hs_2
$$

$$
1+hs_3
$$

and so forth.

---

# From Annual Growth Factors to Long-Term Growth

A rare allele experiences a sequence of growth factors:

$$
(1+hs_1)(1+hs_2)(1+hs_3)\cdots
$$

A favorable year may increase the allele, while an unfavorable year may decrease it.

Therefore, focusing on a single generation does not tell us whether the allele will ultimately persist.

After $T$ generations,

$$
p_T=p_0\prod_{t=1}^{T}(1+hs_t).
$$

Taking logarithms converts the product into a sum:

$$
\ln(p_T)=\ln(p_0)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(1+hs_t).
$$

Dividing by the number of generations gives

$$
\frac{\ln(p_T)-\ln(p_0)}{T}=
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(1+hs_t).
$$

The right-hand side is simply the average logarithmic growth rate experienced by the rare allele.

As the number of generations becomes very large,

$$
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(1+hs_t)
\rightarrow
E[\ln(1+hs)].
$$

The quantity

$$
E[\ln(1+hs)]
$$

is therefore the long-term average logarithmic growth rate of a rare allele.

Its sign determines the evolutionary outcome:

- if $E[\ln(1+hs)] > 0$, the rare allele increases
- if $E[\ln(1+hs)] < 0$, the rare allele declines
- if $E[\ln(1+hs)] = 0$, the rare allele neither increases nor decreases

---

# Returning to the Original Fitness Scale

Logarithmic growth rates are mathematically convenient but difficult to interpret biologically.

To return to the original multiplicative fitness scale, we exponentiate:

$$
G_i=e^{E[\ln(1+hs)]}.
$$

The quantity

$$
G_i
$$

is called the geometric mean fitness of a rare allele.

Biologically:

- $G_i > 1$ means long-term growth
- $G_i < 1$ means long-term decline
- $G_i = 1$ means no net change

---

# Reintroducing Wet and Dry Years

Suppose wet years occur with frequency

$$
f_{wet}
$$

and dry years occur with frequency

$$
f_{dry}
$$

where

$$
f_{wet}+f_{dry}=1.
$$

A rare allele experiences two possible growth factors:

$$
1+hs_{wet}
$$

and

$$
1+hs_{dry}.
$$

The expected logarithmic growth rate is

$$
E[\ln(1+hs)]=
f_{wet}\ln(1+hs_{wet})
+
f_{dry}\ln(1+hs_{dry}).
$$

Substituting into

$$
G_i=e^{E[\ln(1+hs)]}
$$

gives

$$
G_i=
e^{f_{wet}\ln(1+hs_{wet})+f_{dry}\ln(1+hs_{dry})}.
$$

Using

$$
e^{a+b}=e^ae^b
$$

and

$$
e^{c\ln(x)}=x^c
$$

yields

$$
G_i=
(1+hs_{wet})^{f_{wet}}
(1+hs_{dry})^{f_{dry}}.
$$

Thus geometric mean fitness is a weighted geometric average of the growth factors experienced in each environment.

---

# Protected Polymorphism

A polymorphism is protected when each allele can increase when rare.

Using geometric mean fitness, the criteria become:

$$
G_i>1
$$

and

$$
G_j>1.
$$

If both conditions hold:

- neither fixation state is stable
- each allele can invade when rare
- a protected polymorphism exists

---

# Why Is the Rare $\alpha_j$ Case Different?

When $\alpha_j$ is rare,

$$
q\approx0
$$

and

$$
p\approx1.
$$

Almost every copy of $\alpha_j$ occurs in heterozygotes.

The population, however, is dominated by $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ homozygotes.

Therefore the success of the rare allele must be measured relative to the resident population.

The relevant growth factor is

$$
\frac{w_{ij}}{w_{ii}}=
\frac{1+hs}{1+s}.
$$

This expression is approximately the marginal fitness of the rare allele divided by population mean fitness.

It therefore measures whether the rare allele grows faster or slower than the population around it.

---

# Weak-Selection Approximation

For small values of $s$,

$$
\frac{1+hs}{1+s}
\approx
1-(1-h)s.
$$

This approximation follows from:

$$
\frac{1}{1+s}\approx1-s.
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{1+hs}{1+s}
\approx
(1+hs)(1-s)=
1-s+hs-hs^2.
$$

Ignoring the tiny term proportional to $s^2$ gives

$$
1-(1-h)s.
$$

---

# Example 1: An Unprotected Temporal Polymorphism

Suppose

$$
h=\frac34
$$

$$
s_{wet}=0.8
$$

$$
s_{dry}=-0.8
$$

with

$$
f_{wet}=0.5
$$

and

$$
f_{dry}=0.5.
$$

For a rare $\alpha_i$,

$$
g_{wet}=1.6
$$

$$
g_{dry}=0.4.
$$

Thus

$$
G_i=(1.6)^{0.5}(0.4)^{0.5}
=\sqrt{0.64}
=0.80.
$$

Because

$$
G_i<1,
$$

a rare $\alpha_i$ declines.

No protected polymorphism exists.

---

# Example 2: A Protected Temporal Polymorphism

Now suppose

$$
f_{wet}=0.8
$$

and

$$
f_{dry}=0.2.
$$

For rare $\alpha_i$:

$$
G_i=(1.6)^{0.8}(0.4)^{0.2}=1.21.
$$

Thus rare $\alpha_i$ increases.

For rare $\alpha_j$:

$$
g_{wet}=
\frac{1.6}{1.8}=
0.889
$$

$$
g_{dry}=
\frac{0.4}{0.2}=
2.0.
$$

Therefore

$$
G_j=(0.889)^{0.8}(2.0)^{0.2}=1.04.
$$

Thus rare $\alpha_j$ also increases.

Since

$$
G_i>1
$$

and

$$
G_j>1,
$$

a protected polymorphism exists.

---

# Temporal Marginal Overdominance

Notice what happened in Example 2.

Neither homozygote is consistently superior.

Instead:

- $\alpha_i$ is favored in some environments
- $\alpha_j$ is favored in other environments
- each allele gains an advantage when rare

The resulting rare-allele advantage emerges through the marginal fitnesses of the alleles rather than through a permanently superior heterozygote.

This phenomenon is called **temporal marginal overdominance**.

True overdominance is not required.

---

# Comparing Spatial and Temporal Environmental Variation

## Temporal Variation

In temporal models:

- environments occur sequentially
- every individual experiences the same environment in a given generation
- fitness accumulates through time
- geometric mean fitness determines success

| Generation | Environment |
|---|---|
| 1 | Wet |
| 2 | Dry |
| 3 | Wet |
| 4 | Dry |

Only one environment exists at a time.

---

## Spatial Variation

In spatial models:

- environments exist simultaneously
- different habitats favor different alleles
- migration links habitats
- local refuges protect rare alleles

| Habitat | Favored Allele |
|---|---|
| Wet habitat | $\alpha_i$ |
| Dry habitat | $\alpha_j$ |

Multiple environments coexist simultaneously.

---

# Why Spatial Variation Often Maintains More Variation

Temporal environments provide:

$$
\text{One environment at a time}.
$$

Spatial environments provide:

$$
\text{Multiple environments simultaneously}.
$$

Because favorable habitats continue to exist somewhere in space, rare alleles can persist even when selected against elsewhere.

Spatial heterogeneity therefore often produces stronger balancing selection than temporal heterogeneity.

# Closing Thought: The Legacy and Limitations of this Model

This model draws from work by Dempster (1955), Haldane & Jayakar (1963) and  Gillespie (1973/1974). The model was historically important because it represents rigorous attempts to determine whether fluctuating environments could maintain genetic variation.

However, the model makes several restrictive assumptions:

- all individuals experience the same environment each generation
- generations do not overlap
- no seed banks or dormancy exist
- there are no spatial refuges
- dominance remains fixed
- environmental effects act only through changes in selection coefficients

Under these assumptions, most authors have concluded that stable temporal polymorphisms occupy a relatively small region of parameter space. In other words, temporal environmental variation alone does not automatically generate balancing selection.

Modern empirical and theoretical work paints a more complicated picture. Genomic studies have uncovered many candidate loci showing signatures of temporal balancing selection, and newer models have identified mechanisms that can greatly strengthen polymorphism maintenance. These include:

- reversal of dominance
- genotype-by-environment interactions
- the storage effect
- seed banks and dormant life stages
- overlapping generations
- combined spatial and temporal environmental variation

Consequently, the modern view is not that temporal balancing selection is rare. Rather, the model showed the limitations of one particularly simple form of environmental fluctuation. Later theory demonstrated that when additional biological realism is included, temporally maintained polymorphisms can be much more common than the model originally predicted.

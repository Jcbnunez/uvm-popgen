# Historical Motivation

One of the central questions in population genetics is:

> Why does genetic variation persist?

If one allele is consistently favored, directional selection generally drives that allele toward fixation. However, natural populations often maintain substantial genetic variation.

Several mechanisms can maintain polymorphism:

- mutation-selection balance
- migration-selection balance
- negative frequency-dependent selection
- overdominance
- spatially varying environments
- temporally varying environments

Today we examine whether fluctuating environments alone can maintain a stable polymorphism.

---

# Biological Scenario

Consider a single locus, $\alpha$.

Two alleles segregate:

$$
\alpha_i
$$

and

$$
\alpha_j
$$

with frequencies

$$
p=f(\alpha_i)
$$

and

$$
q=f(\alpha_j).
$$

Because only two alleles exist,

$$
p+q=1
$$

and therefore

$$
q=1-p.
$$

The environment changes among generations.

| Generation | Favored Allele |
|---|---|
| Wet Year | $\alpha_i$ |
| Dry Year | $\alpha_j$ |

Thus selection fluctuates through time.

---

# Model Assumptions

1. Random mating.
2. Infinite population size.
3. No mutation.
4. No migration.
5. No drift.
6. Discrete generations.
7. Everyone experiences the same environment each generation.
8. Dominance remains constant.
9. Selection fluctuates among generations.

---

# Fitness Parameterization

We model fitnesses using a selection coefficient $s_t$ and a dominance coefficient $h$:

$$
w_{ii,t}=1+s_t
$$

$$
w_{ij,t}=1+hs_t
$$

$$
w_{jj,t}=1.
$$

Interpretation:

- $s_t > 0$ favors $\alpha_i$
- $s_t < 0$ favors $\alpha_j$

Thus environmental change acts through changes in $s_t$.

---

# Genotype Frequencies Before Selection

Under random mating:

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_i:p_t^2
$$

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_j:2p_tq_t
$$

$$
\alpha_j\alpha_j:q_t^2.
$$

After selection:

$$
p_t^2(1+s_t)
$$

$$
2p_tq_t(1+hs_t)
$$

$$
q_t^2.
$$

---

# Deriving Mean Fitness

Mean fitness is the sum of genotype frequencies weighted by fitness:

$$
\bar w_t=p_t^2(1+s_t)+2p_tq_t(1+hs_t)+q_t^2.
$$

Expanding:

$$
\bar w_t=p_t^2+p_t^2s_t+2p_tq_t+2hp_tq_ts_t+q_t^2.
$$

Using

$$
p_t^2+2p_tq_t+q_t^2=1
$$

gives

$$
\bar w_t=1+s_t(p_t^2+2hp_tq_t).
$$

---

# Marginal Fitness

Selection acts on genotypes, but evolution is measured through allele frequencies. Therefore we need a quantity describing how well an allele performs across all genetic backgrounds in which it occurs.

That quantity is the **marginal fitness**.

Starting with

$$
p'=\frac{p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}}{\bar w},
$$

factor out $p$:

$$
p'=\frac{p(pw_{ii}+qw_{ij})}{\bar w}.
$$

Define

$$
w_{\alpha_i}=pw_{ii}+qw_{ij}.
$$

Similarly,

$$
w_{\alpha_j}=pw_{ij}+qw_{jj}.
$$

Thus

$$
p'=\frac{pw_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}
$$

and

$$
q'=\frac{qw_{\alpha_j}}{\bar w}.
$$

Marginal fitness measures allele success.

Mean fitness measures population success.

---

# Why Marginal Fitness Determines Evolution

Starting from

$$
p'=\frac{pw_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}
$$

divide by $p$:

$$
\frac{p'}{p}=\frac{w_{\alpha_i}}{\bar w}.
$$

Therefore:

- if $w_{\alpha_i} > \bar w$, allele $\alpha_i$ increases
- if $w_{\alpha_i} < \bar w$, allele $\alpha_i$ decreases

Evolution depends on marginal fitness relative to mean fitness.

---

# Marginal Fitness Under This Model

Substituting genotype fitnesses:

For $\alpha_i$:

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=p_t(1+s_t)+q_t(1+hs_t)
$$

which simplifies to

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=1+s_t(p_t+hq_t).
$$

For $\alpha_j$:

$$
w_{\alpha_j,t}=p_t(1+hs_t)+q_t
$$

which simplifies to

$$
w_{\alpha_j,t}=1+hp_ts_t.
$$

---

# Allele-Frequency Recursion

Substituting the marginal-fitness expressions:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p_t[1+s_t(p_t+hq_t)]}
{1+s_t(p_t^2+2hp_tq_t)}.
$$

This recursion completely describes deterministic allele-frequency change.

---

# Why Do We Focus on Rare Alleles?

Balancing selection is fundamentally about protection from extinction.

A common allele is not in immediate danger of being lost because many copies remain in the population.

A rare allele, in contrast, is at risk of disappearing entirely.

Therefore the key question is:

> Can an allele recover when it becomes rare?

A protected polymorphism exists only if both alleles can increase from rarity.

---

# Rare Allele Dynamics

Suppose $\alpha_i$ is rare.

Then

$$
p \approx 0
$$

and

$$
q \approx 1.
$$

Substituting into

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}=1+s_t(p_t+hq_t)
$$

gives

$$
w_{\alpha_i,t}\approx1+hs_t.
$$

Thus a rare $\alpha_i$ behaves approximately like a heterozygote.

---

# Defining a Growth Factor

To simplify notation, define

$$
g_t=1+hs_t.
$$

The quantity $g_t$ is called the **per-generation growth factor** of a rare allele.

Interpretation:

- $g_t > 1$ means the allele increases
- $g_t < 1$ means the allele decreases
- $g_t = 1$ means no change

For example:

$$
g_t=1.20
$$

means the allele increases by 20% that generation.

Lowercase $g$ always refers to growth during a single generation.

---

# From Annual Growth Factors to Long-Term Growth

A rare allele experiences the sequence

$$
g_1,g_2,g_3,\ldots
$$

through time.

After $T$ generations:

$$
p_T=p_0\prod_{t=1}^{T}g_t.
$$

Taking logarithms:

$$
\ln(p_T)=\ln(p_0)+\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(g_t).
$$

Dividing by $T$:

$$
\frac{\ln(p_T)-\ln(p_0)}{T}=
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(g_t).
$$

The right-hand side is the average logarithmic growth rate observed across generations.

As $T$ becomes large,

$$
\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}\ln(g_t)
\rightarrow
E[\ln(g)].
$$

The quantity

$$
E[\ln(g)]
$$

is therefore the long-term average logarithmic growth rate.

---

# Returning to the Original Fitness Scale

We would like growth measured on the original multiplicative scale.

Exponentiating gives

$$
G_i=e^{E[\ln(g)]}.
$$

Uppercase $G$ denotes the **geometric mean fitness** of a rare allele.

Interpretation:

$$
G_i>1
$$

means long-term growth.

$$
G_i<1
$$

means long-term decline.

$$
G_i=1
$$

means no net change.

A useful distinction is:

$$
g=\text{one-generation growth factor}
$$

$$
G=\text{long-term geometric mean fitness}.
$$

---

# Reintroducing Wet and Dry Years

Suppose wet years occur with frequency

$$
f_{wet}
$$

and dry years occur with frequency

$$
f_{dry}
$$

such that

$$
f_{wet}+f_{dry}=1.
$$

Define

$$
g_{wet}=1+hs_{wet}
$$

and

$$
g_{dry}=1+hs_{dry}.
$$

The expected logarithmic growth rate is

$$
E[\ln(g)]=
f_{wet}\ln(g_{wet})
+
f_{dry}\ln(g_{dry}).
$$

Substituting into

$$
G_i=e^{E[\ln(g)]}
$$

gives

$$
G_i=
g_{wet}^{f_{wet}}
g_{dry}^{f_{dry}}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
G_i=
(1+hs_{wet})^{f_{wet}}
(1+hs_{dry})^{f_{dry}}.
$$

Thus geometric mean fitness is a weighted geometric average of environmental growth factors.

---

# Protected Polymorphism

A polymorphism is protected if both alleles increase when rare.

Therefore:

$$
G_i>1
$$

and

$$
G_j>1.
$$

If both conditions hold:

- each allele can invade
- neither fixation state is stable
- a protected polymorphism exists

---

# Why Is the Rare $\alpha_j$ Case Different?

When $\alpha_j$ is rare:

$$
q\approx0
$$

and

$$
p\approx1.
$$

Almost all copies of $\alpha_j$ occur in heterozygotes, but the population is dominated by $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ homozygotes.

The relevant growth factor is therefore

$$
\frac{w_{ij}}{w_{ii}}=
\frac{1+hs}{1+s}.
$$

This quantity is approximately the marginal fitness of the rare allele divided by the population mean fitness.

It measures whether the rare allele outgrows the resident population.

---

# Geometric Mean Fitness of a Rare $\alpha_j$

Define

$$
g_{wet}=
\frac{1+hs_{wet}}
{1+s_{wet}}
$$

and

$$
g_{dry}=
\frac{1+hs_{dry}}
{1+s_{dry}}.
$$

These are the per-generation growth factors for a rare $\alpha_j$.

Its geometric mean fitness is

$$
G_j=
g_{wet}^{f_{wet}}
g_{dry}^{f_{dry}}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
G_j=
\left(
\frac{1+hs_{wet}}
{1+s_{wet}}
\right)^{f_{wet}}
\left(
\frac{1+hs_{dry}}
{1+s_{dry}}
\right)^{f_{dry}}.
$$

Interpretation:

$$
G_j>1
$$

means invasion,

$$
G_j<1
$$

means decline.

---

# Example 1: An Unprotected Temporal Polymorphism

Suppose

$$
h=\frac34
$$

$$
s_{wet}=0.8
$$

$$
s_{dry}=-0.8
$$

with

$$
f_{wet}=0.5
$$

and

$$
f_{dry}=0.5.
$$

For rare $\alpha_i$:

$$
g_{wet}=1.6
$$

$$
g_{dry}=0.4.
$$

Therefore

$$
G_i=(1.6)^{0.5}(0.4)^{0.5}=0.80.
$$

Because

$$
G_i<1,
$$

a rare $\alpha_i$ declines.

The polymorphism is not protected.

---

# Example 2: A Protected Temporal Polymorphism

Suppose

$$
f_{wet}=0.8
$$

and

$$
f_{dry}=0.2.
$$

For rare $\alpha_i$:

$$
G_i=(1.6)^{0.8}(0.4)^{0.2}=1.21.
$$

For rare $\alpha_j$:

$$
g_{wet}=\frac{1.6}{1.8}=0.889
$$

$$
g_{dry}=\frac{0.4}{0.2}=2.0.
$$

Therefore

$$
G_j=(0.889)^{0.8}(2.0)^{0.2}=1.04.
$$

Since

$$
G_i>1
$$

and

$$
G_j>1,
$$

each allele can invade when rare and a protected polymorphism exists.

---

# Temporal Marginal Overdominance

Notice what happened.

Neither homozygote is always superior.

Instead:

- $\alpha_i$ is favored in some environments
- $\alpha_j$ is favored in others
- each allele gains an advantage when rare

The rare-allele advantage emerges through marginal fitnesses rather than through a permanently superior heterozygote.

This phenomenon is called **temporal marginal overdominance**.

---

# Comparing Temporal and Spatial Variation

## Temporal Variation

- one environment at a time
- everyone experiences that environment
- fitness accumulates through time
- geometric mean fitness determines success

## Spatial Variation

- multiple environments coexist
- migration links habitats
- local refuges protect diversity

Because favorable habitats persist somewhere in space, spatial variation often maintains polymorphism more effectively than temporal variation.

(1+hs_{wet})^{f_{wet}}

$$
G_j=
\left(
\frac{1+hs_{wet}}
{1+s_{wet}}
\right)^{f_{wet}}
\left(
\frac{1+hs_{dry}}
{1+s_{dry}}
\right)^{f_{dry}}.
$$

7. Protected polymorphism requires

$$
G_i>1
$$

and

$$
G_j>1.
$$

8. Temporal marginal overdominance occurs when both alleles gain an advantage when rare.

# Closing Thought: The Legacy and Limitations of this Model

This model draws from work by Dempster (1955), Haldane & Jayakar (1963) and  Gillespie (1973/1974). The model was historically important because it represents rigorous attempts to determine whether fluctuating environments could maintain genetic variation.

However, the model makes several restrictive assumptions:

- all individuals experience the same environment each generation
- generations do not overlap
- no seed banks or dormancy exist
- there are no spatial refuges
- dominance remains fixed
- environmental effects act only through changes in selection coefficients

Under these assumptions, most authors have concluded that stable temporal polymorphisms occupy a relatively small region of parameter space. In other words, temporal environmental variation alone does not automatically generate balancing selection.

Modern empirical and theoretical work paints a more complicated picture. Genomic studies have uncovered many candidate loci showing signatures of temporal balancing selection, and newer models have identified mechanisms that can greatly strengthen polymorphism maintenance. These include:

- reversal of dominance
- genotype-by-environment interactions
- the storage effect
- seed banks and dormant life stages
- overlapping generations
- combined spatial and temporal environmental variation

Consequently, the modern view is not that temporal balancing selection is rare. Rather, the model showed the limitations of one particularly simple form of environmental fluctuation. Later theory demonstrated that when additional biological realism is included, temporally maintained polymorphisms can be much more common than the model originally predicted.

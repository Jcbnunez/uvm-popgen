# Migration-Selection Equilibrium

---

# The Fundamental Question

Selection and migration often act in opposite directions.

- Selection favors locally adapted alleles.

- Migration introduces alleles from other populations.

- The resulting allele frequency reflects a balance between these forces.

This balance is known as **migration-selection equilibrium**.

---

# Example: Two Populations

Consider two populations:

Population 1:

- Environment favors allele $i$

Population 2:

- Environment favors allele $j$

Migration occurs between populations.

```text
Population 1 <---- m ----> Population 2
       i                       j
```

Selection increases local adaptation.

Migration homogenizes populations.

The observed allele frequency depends on which force is stronger.

---

# Migration Changes Allele Frequencies

Suppose the frequency of allele i is

$$
p
$$

and migrants arrive from another population where allele $i$ occurs at frequency

$$
p_m.
$$

If migration occurs at rate

$$
m,
$$

then after migration the allele frequency becomes

$$
p' = (1-m)p + mp_m.
$$

Expanding,

$$
p' = p - mp + mp_m
$$

and rearranging,

$$
p' = p + m(p_m-p).
$$

Migration therefore pulls allele frequencies toward the source population.

---

# Selection Changes Allele Frequencies

Suppose allele i has a selective advantage

$$
s
$$

Fitnesses are:

$$
x_{ii} = 1+s
$$

$$
x_{ij} = 1+\frac{s}{2}
$$

$$
x_{jj} = 1.
$$

Selection increases the frequency of allele i each generation.

The larger

$$
s
$$

is, the faster allele i spreads.

---

# Selection Versus Migration

Migration introduces maladaptive alleles.

Selection removes them.

```text

Migration
i ------------> j

Selection
j ------------> i

```

Migration acts to reduce differences among populations.

Selection acts to increase differences among populations.

---

# One-Way Migration Model

Consider an island receiving migrants from a very large mainland population.

```text
Mainland ------------> Island
               m
```

Suppose:

- Allele j is common on the mainland.
- Allele i is favored on the island.

Migration continually introduces j.
Selection continually removes j.

Eventually an equilibrium is reached.

---

# Migration-Selection Equilibrium

At equilibrium,

$$
\Delta p = 0.
$$

The gain from selection is exactly balanced by the loss from migration.

Allele frequencies stop changing even though migration and selection continue operating every generation.

---

# Derivation of the Equilibrium Frequency

# Migration-Selection Equilibrium

---

# Motivation

Last lecture, we derived how migration changes allele frequencies.

Recall the migration equation:

$$

p' = (1-m)p + mp_m

$$

where

- $p$ is the allele frequency before migration

- $p_m$ is the allele frequency in migrants

- $m$ is the migration rate

We found that migration pulls populations toward one another by introducing alleles from elsewhere.

Today we ask a deeper question:

> What happens when migration introduces maladaptive alleles, but selection continually removes them?

This conflict between migration and selection is one of the fundamental problems in evolutionary biology.

---

# A Simple Biological Example

Imagine two populations connected by migration.

Population 1 occupies a cold environment.

Population 2 occupies a warm environment.

Suppose:

- Allele $i$ increases fitness in cold environments.

- Allele $j$ increases fitness in warm environments.

Migration occurs between populations.

```text

Cold Population <---- m ----> Warm Population

i j

```

Migration introduces alleles adapted to the wrong environment.

Selection favors locally beneficial alleles.

The resulting allele frequency reflects a balance between these opposing forces.

This balance is called **migration-selection equilibrium**.

---

# Island-Mainland Model

To simplify the mathematics, consider a classic population genetics model.

```text

Mainland -----------> Island

m

```

Assumptions:

- The mainland is extremely large.

- The island is smaller.

- Migration occurs only from mainland to island.

- Allele j is common on the mainland.

- Allele i is favored on the island.

Migration continually introduces allele j.

Selection continually removes allele j.

---

# Defining the Problem

Let

$$
q
$$

be the frequency of allele $j$ on the island.

Therefore,

$$
p = 1-q
$$

is the frequency of allele $i$.

Our goal is to determine:

> What value of $q$ will persist indefinitely when migration and selection occur every generation?

---

### Step 1: Effect of Migration

From last lecture:

$$
q' = (1-m)q + mq_m
$$

Suppose the mainland is fixed for allele $j$:

$$
q_m = 1.
$$

Substituting:

$$
q' = (1-m)q + m(1)
$$

Expanding:

$$
q' = q - mq + m
$$

Factoring:

$$
q' = q + m(1-q)
$$

Therefore the change caused by migration is

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=q'-q
$$

Substituting,

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=[q+m(1-q)]-q
$$

The q terms cancel:

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=m(1-q).
$$

---

### Interpreting the Migration Term

The migration contribution is

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=m(1-q).
$$

Notice:

If

$$
q=0
$$

then

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=m.
$$

Migration increases allele frequency rapidly.

If

$$
q=1
$$

then

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=0.
$$

Migration has no effect because the allele is already fixed.

Thus migration becomes less effective as the immigrant allele becomes more common.
# General Migration-Selection Equilibrium with Dominance

Suppose allele i is favored on the island and allele j is maladaptive.

Let

$$
p = \text{freq}(i)
$$

$$
q = \text{freq}(j)
$$

with

$$
p+q=1.
$$

Now introduce a general dominance coefficient

$$
h.
$$

Fitnesses become

$$
w_{ii}=1
$$

$$
w_{ij}=1-hs
$$

$$
w_{jj}=1-s
$$

Special cases:

| h | Interpretation |
|---|---|
| 0 | j is completely recessive |
| 1/2 | additive |
| 1 | j is completely dominant |

---

# Frequency of Allele i After Selection

From viability selection,

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2w_{ii}+pqw_{ij}}
{\bar w}.
$$

Substituting fitnesses:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2+pq(1-hs)}
{\bar w}.
$$

Expand:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p^2+pq-hspq}
{\bar w}.
$$

Factor out p:

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p(p+q)-hspq}
{\bar w}.
$$

Since

$$
p+q=1,
$$

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p-hspq}
{\bar w}.
$$

---

# Mean Fitness

By definition

$$
\bar w=
p^2w_{ii}
+
2pqw_{ij}
+
q^2w_{jj}.
$$

Substituting:

$$
\bar w=
p^2
+
2pq(1-hs)
+
q^2(1-s).
$$

Expand:

$$
\bar w=
p^2+2pq-2hspq+q^2-sq^2.
$$

Using

$$
p^2+2pq+q^2=1
$$

gives

$$
\bar w=
1-2hspq-sq^2.
$$

Factor q:

$$
\bar w=
1-sq(2hp+q).
$$

This is the exact mean fitness.

---

# Substitute Back

Therefore

$$
p_{t+1}=
\frac{p-hspq}
{1-sq(2hp+q)}.
$$

This is the exact recursion for allele i.

---

# Change in Allele Frequency

The change due to selection is

$$
\Delta p=
p_{t+1}-p.
$$

Substituting,

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{p-hspq}
{1-sq(2hp+q)}-p
$$

Place over a common denominator:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
p-hspq-p[1-sq(2hp+q)]
}
{1-sq(2hp+q)}.
$$

Expand:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
p-hspq-p+spq(2hp+q)
}{1-sq(2hp+q)}.
$$

The p terms cancel:

$$
\Delta p=
\frac{
spq(2hp+q-h)
}
{1-sq(2hp+q)}.
$$

This is the exact change due to viability selection.

---

# Weak Selection Approximation

If

$$
s \ll 1
$$

then

$$
1-sq(2hp+q)
\approx 1.
$$

Therefore

$$
\Delta p
\approx
spq(2hp+q-h).
$$

Substituting

$$
p=1-q
$$

gives

$$
\Delta p
\approx
spq[h+(1-2h)q].
$$

This is the standard selection equation with dominance.

---

# Change in the Deleterious Allele

Since

$$
\Delta q=-\Delta p,
$$

we obtain

$$
\Delta q
\approx
-spq[h+(1-2h)q].
$$

This is the general selection term for any dominance coefficient.

---
# Add Migration

From the previous lecture,

$$
\Delta q_{mig}=
m(1-q).
$$

Under complete recessivity,

$$
h=0.
$$

The selection term becomes

$$
\Delta q_{sel}=
-spq[0+(1-0)q].
$$

Simplifying,

$$
\Delta q_{sel}=
-spq^2.
$$

Since

$$
p=1-q,
$$

$$
\Delta q_{sel}=
-sq^2(1-q).
$$

Combining migration and selection:

$$
\Delta q=
m(1-q)-sq^2(1-q).
$$

Factoring out

$$
(1-q),
$$

$$
\Delta q=
(1-q)\Big[m-sq^2\Big].
$$

This is the migration-selection equation when the deleterious allele is completely recessive.

---

# Equilibrium

At equilibrium,

$$
\Delta q=0.
$$

Therefore,

$$
(1-q)\Big[m-sq^2\Big]=0.
$$

The trivial solution is

$$
q=1.
$$

The biologically relevant equilibrium comes from

$$
m-sq^2=0.
$$

Rearranging,

$$
m=sq^2.
$$

Divide by s:

$$
\frac{m}{s}=
q^2.
$$

Taking the square root,

$$
q^*=
\sqrt{\frac{m}{s}}.
$$

This is the equilibrium equation for a completely recessive deleterious immigrant allele.

---

# Conditions for Local Adaptation

Local adaptation is maintained when

$$
s>m.
$$

Selection removes maladaptive migrants faster than migration introduces them.

If

$$
m>s,
$$

migration overwhelms selection.

Populations become more genetically similar.

---

# Critical Migration Threshold

A useful approximation is

$$
m_{crit}\approx s.
$$

When migration exceeds this threshold, local adaptation becomes difficult to maintain.

This phenomenon is known as **gene swamping**.

---

# Gene Swamping

Imagine an allele that is beneficial locally.

```text

Selection

i -> i -> i -> i

Migration

j -> j -> j -> j

```

If migrants arrive too frequently:

- Local adaptation cannot become established.

- Beneficial alleles remain rare.

- Genetic divergence decreases.

---

# Selection-Migration Balance and FST

Migration and selection strongly influence

$$
F_{ST}.
$$

High migration:

- Low differentiation

- Low FST

Low migration:

- High differentiation

- High FST

Strong local selection can maintain elevated FST despite migration.

---

# Genomic Consequences

Loci experiencing divergent selection often show:

- Elevated FST

- Reduced effective migration

- Large allele frequency differences

- Strong LD near adaptive loci

These regions may appear as genomic islands of divergence.

---

# Migration-Selection Balance Across the Genome

Not all loci experience the same equilibrium.

Neutral loci:

- Strongly influenced by migration

- Weakly affected by selection

Selected loci:

- Resist homogenization

- Remain differentiated

```text

Neutral loci -> Similar populations

Adaptive loci -> Divergent populations

```

---

# Example: Latitudinal Clines in Drosophila

In Drosophila:

- Migration occurs among populations.

- Temperature varies with latitude.

- Different alleles are favored in different climates.

Migration homogenizes populations.

Selection maintains geographic differentiation.

The observed clines represent migration-selection equilibrium.

---

# Example: Marine-Freshwater Systems

Many species inhabit:

```text

Marine Habitat <----> Freshwater Habitat

```

Migration occurs continuously.

Different environments favor different alleles.

Stable allele-frequency differences persist when migration is balanced by selection.

---

# Key Insight

Migration-selection equilibrium occurs when

$$
\text{Selection Gain}=
\text{Migration Loss}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\Delta p = 0.
$$

The equilibrium frequency depends on only two parameters:

- Migration rate, m

- Selection coefficient, s

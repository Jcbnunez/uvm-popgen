# Introduction

In the previous lecture we examined selective sweeps, where directional selection pushes a beneficial allele toward fixation.

Under a selective sweep:

- One allele wins.
- Alternative alleles are removed.
- Genetic diversity decreases.
- Genealogies become shallow.

Balancing selection produces the opposite long-term effect.

Rather than eliminating variation, balancing selection actively maintains variation.

As a consequence, balancing selection leaves genomic footprints that are nearly the reverse of those produced by recent positive selection.

The central question becomes:

> What does the genome look like when natural selection maintains multiple alleles for long periods of time?

---

# What is Balancing Selection?

Balancing selection refers to any selective process that maintains multiple alleles within a population longer than expected under neutrality.

Under neutrality:

- Some alleles are lost by drift.
- Some alleles become fixed by drift.
- Heterozygosity gradually disappears.

Under balancing selection:

- Multiple alleles are preserved.
- Polymorphisms can persist for thousands or millions of generations.
- Genetic diversity is elevated rather than reduced.

Common mechanisms include:

1. Heterozygote advantage (overdominance)
2. Negative frequency-dependent selection
3. Spatially varying selection
4. Temporally varying selection

---

# Example: Heterozygote Advantage

Suppose a locus $\alpha$ contains two alleles:

- $\alpha_i$
- $\alpha_j$

Assume genotype fitnesses are

| Genotype | Fitness |
|-----------|-----------|
| $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ | $1-s$ |
| $\alpha_i\alpha_j$ | $1$ |
| $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ | $1-t$ |

where

- $s > 0$
- $t > 0$

Both homozygotes suffer fitness reductions.

The heterozygote has the highest fitness.

This is called overdominance.

---

# Why Neither Allele Can Fix

Suppose allele $\alpha_i$ becomes very rare.

Most copies of $\alpha_i$ occur in heterozygotes:

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_j
$$

Because heterozygotes have the highest fitness, selection favors the rare allele.

Now suppose $\alpha_i$ becomes extremely common.

Many copies now occur in

$$
\alpha_i\alpha_i
$$

which has reduced fitness.

Selection therefore favors $\alpha_j$.

Whenever either allele becomes too common, selection pushes it back down.

Whenever either allele becomes too rare, selection pushes it back up.

This creates a stable equilibrium.

---

# Deriving the Equilibrium Frequency

Let

$$
p = \text{frequency of } \alpha_i
$$

and

$$
q = 1-p
$$

At equilibrium, neither allele changes in frequency.

For overdominance, the equilibrium frequency of allele $\alpha_i$ is

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

and the equilibrium frequency of allele $\alpha_j$ is

$$
\hat{q} = \frac{s_{ii}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

### Derivation

At equilibrium, the two alleles must have equal marginal fitness. If one allele had higher marginal fitness than the other, its frequency would continue to increase, so equilibrium could not be maintained.

The marginal fitness of allele $\alpha_i$ is

$$
w_i = p(1-s_{ii}) + q(1)
$$

Substituting $q = 1-p$ gives

$$
w_i = p(1-s_{ii}) + (1-p)
$$

Expanding:

$$
w_i = p - s_{ii}p + 1 - p
$$

Therefore,

$$
w_i = 1 - s_{ii}p
$$

The marginal fitness of allele $\alpha_j$ is

$$
w_j = p(1) + (1-p)(1-s_{jj})
$$

Expanding the second term:

$$
w_j = p + 1 - p - s_{jj} + s_{jj}p
$$

Therefore,

$$
w_j = 1 - s_{jj} + s_{jj}p
$$

At equilibrium,

$$
w_i = w_j
$$

Substituting the expressions above:

$$
1 - s_{ii}p = 1 - s_{jj} + s_{jj}p
$$

Subtracting 1 from both sides:

$$
-s_{ii}p = -s_{jj} + s_{jj}p
$$

Multiplying both sides by $-1$:

$$
s_{ii}p = s_{jj} - s_{jj}p
$$

Adding $s_{jj}p$ to both sides:

$$
s_{ii}p + s_{jj}p = s_{jj}
$$

Factoring out $p$:

$$
p(s_{ii} + s_{jj}) = s_{jj}
$$

Solving for $p$:

$$
\hat{p} = \frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

Because allele frequencies must sum to one,

$$
\hat{q} = 1 - \hat{p}
$$

Substituting the expression for $\hat{p}$:

$$
\hat{q} = 1 - \frac{s_{jj}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

Combining terms:

$$
\hat{q} = \frac{s_{ii} + s_{jj} - s_{jj}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

Thus,

$$
\hat{q} = \frac{s_{ii}}{s_{ii} + s_{jj}}
$$

### Biological Interpretation

The equilibrium frequency of an allele is determined by the fitness cost of the *opposite* homozygote.

- If $s_{jj}$ is large, genotype $\alpha_j\alpha_j$ is strongly selected against, so $\alpha_i$ is maintained at a higher equilibrium frequency.
- If $s_{ii}$ is large, genotype $\alpha_i\alpha_i$ is strongly selected against, so $\alpha_j$ is maintained at a higher equilibrium frequency.
- If $s_{ii} = s_{jj}$, then  both alleles are maintained at equal frequency.

This stable equilibrium is the hallmark of heterozygote advantage and explains how balancing selection can maintain genetic variation indefinitely in large populations.

# Balancing Selection Maintains Deep Genealogies

Under a sweep, all chromosomes trace back to a recent ancestor.

Under balancing selection, two or more allelic classes persist for very long periods.

This fundamentally changes the genealogy.

Selective sweep:

```text
       |
       |
       |
       |
       *
      /|\
     / | \
    /  |  \
   *   *   *
```

Balancing selection:

```text
          *
         / \
        /   \
       /     \
      /       \
     *         *
    /|\       /|\
   * * *     * * *
```

The split between allelic classes can persist for extraordinarily long periods.

Consequently, the average coalescent time increases.

---

# Genomic Footprint 1: Elevated Nucleotide Diversity

Recall nucleotide diversity:

$$
\pi = \text{average pairwise sequence difference}
$$

A selective sweep reduces $\pi$ because chromosomes become similar.

Balancing selection does the opposite.

Different allelic classes accumulate mutations independently.

As a result:

- More sequence differences occur among chromosomes.
- Pairwise differences increase.
- Nucleotide diversity rises.

Example:

Without balancing selection

```text
ACCTGA
ACCTGA
ACCTGA
ACCTGG
```

With balancing selection

```text
ACCTGA
ACCTGA

TGGCAT
TGGCAT
```

Differences among chromosomes become much larger.

Therefore:

$$
\pi_{\text{balancing}} > \pi_{\text{neutral}}
$$

---

# Why Diversity Increases

Suppose two alleles have been maintained for 100,000 generations.

Chromosomes carrying $\alpha_i$ accumulate mutations.

Chromosomes carrying $\alpha_j$ also accumulate mutations.

Eventually the two classes diverge substantially.

Every comparison between classes contributes many nucleotide differences.

Thus balancing selection creates local peaks of diversity.

This is the opposite of the valleys generated by selective sweeps.

---

# Genomic Footprint 2: Excess Intermediate-Frequency Alleles

Balancing selection maintains alleles at moderate frequencies.

Therefore mutations linked to those alleles often occur at intermediate frequencies as well.

The site-frequency spectrum becomes enriched for intermediate-frequency variants.

This contrasts with sweeps, which generate excess rare variants.

---

# Tajima's D

One way to quantify this pattern is Tajima's D.

Tajima's D compares:

- nucleotide diversity ($\pi$)
- the number of segregating sites ($S$)

Under neutrality:

$$
D \approx 0
$$

After a sweep:

$$
D < 0
$$

due to excess rare variants.

Under balancing selection:

$$
D > 0
$$

because intermediate-frequency variants become unusually common.

Thus highly positive Tajima's D values often indicate balancing selection.

---

# Genomic Footprint 3: Long-Term Haplotype Divergence

Balancing selection can maintain multiple haplotypes simultaneously.

Imagine two ancient haplotype classes:

```text
AAAAAAAAAAAAAA
AAAAAAAAAAAAAA
AAAAAAAAAAAAAA

GGGGGGGGGGGGGG
GGGGGGGGGGGGGG
GGGGGGGGGGGGGG
```

Within a class:

- chromosomes are similar.

Between classes:

- chromosomes are highly divergent.

The result is structured haplotype variation rather than a single dominant haplotype.

---

# Genomic Footprint 4: Elevated Local Coalescent Times

Under neutrality, lineages coalesce at approximately

$$
2N_e
$$

generations in a diploid population.

Balancing selection can maintain allelic classes much longer.

Consequently:

$$
T_{balancing} \gg 2N_e
$$

Local genealogies become unusually old.

Because mutations accumulate with time, older genealogies contain more variation.

This contributes directly to elevated diversity.

---

# Genomic Footprint 5: Trans-Species Polymorphism

One of the strongest signatures of balancing selection occurs when polymorphisms persist through speciation events.

Suppose two species diverged:

```text
Ancestor
   |
   |
---+---
|     |
Sp1   Sp2
```

If balancing selection maintains the same alleles before and after speciation:

```text
Species 1:
αi
αj

Species 2:
αi
αj
```

the polymorphism is older than the species split itself.

This phenomenon is called a trans-species polymorphism.

---

# Major Example: The MHC

The Major Histocompatibility Complex (MHC) provides one of the strongest known examples.

MHC genes:

- Present pathogen-derived peptides.
- Influence immune recognition.
- Contain extraordinarily high diversity.

Many MHC alleles are older than some species divergences.

As a result:

- Humans may share ancient MHC lineages with other primates.
- Gene trees may not match species trees.
- Diversity is exceptionally high.

This is a classic hallmark of long-term balancing selection.

---

# Frequency-Dependent Selection

Balancing selection does not require heterozygote advantage.

Consider negative frequency-dependent selection.

The fitness of an allele decreases as it becomes common.

When rare:

$$
\text{High fitness}
$$

When common:

$$
\text{Low fitness}
$$

Thus rare alleles are continually favored.

Selection prevents any single allele from dominating.

Genetic variation is preserved indefinitely.

---

# Spatially Variable Selection

Different environments may favor different alleles.

Example:

| Habitat | Favored Allele |
|----------|----------|
| Environment A | $\alpha_i$ |
| Environment B | $\alpha_j$ |

Migration continually mixes populations.

Selection continually favors different alleles in different locations.

The species as a whole can maintain polymorphism for very long periods.

---

# Comparison with Selective Sweeps

| Feature | Selective Sweep | Balancing Selection |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|
| Diversity | Reduced | Elevated |
| Heterozygosity | Reduced | Elevated |
| Genealogies | Shallow | Deep |
| Tajima's D | Negative | Positive |
| Haplotype Pattern | One dominant haplotype | Multiple divergent haplotypes |
| Coalescent Time | Short | Long |
| Intermediate-Frequency Alleles | Reduced | Elevated |
| Trans-Species Polymorphism | No | Possible |

# Why Recombination Matters

Balancing selection directly affects only one locus.

Nearby loci remain linked through linkage disequilibrium.

Recombination gradually breaks these associations.

Therefore:

- Low recombination → wide balancing-selection footprint.
- High recombination → narrow balancing-selection footprint.

Exactly the same principle applies to selective sweeps.

The difference lies in the direction of the signal.

Sweeps create valleys.

Balancing selection creates peaks.

---

# Big Picture

Selective sweeps and balancing selection produce nearly opposite genomic signatures.

A sweep occurs when selection favors one allele and eliminates alternatives.

Balancing selection occurs when selection maintains multiple alleles simultaneously.

As a result, balancing selection produces:

- Elevated nucleotide diversity.
- Excess intermediate-frequency variants.
- Positive Tajima's D.
- Deep genealogies.
- Divergent haplotype classes.
- Elevated coalescent times.
- Occasionally trans-species polymorphisms.

Modern population genomic studies routinely search for these signatures to identify genes involved in host-pathogen interactions, immune function, ecological specialization, and other evolutionary processes where genetic diversity itself is favored by natural selection.
